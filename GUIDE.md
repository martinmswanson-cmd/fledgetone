# Fledgetone guide

## Getting started

Fledgetone is a music maker that runs in your browser. You build a song on the **Arrangement Timeline** out of **tracks** (one instrument each) and **clips** (blocks of notes on a track), mix it, and export it as a WAV file.

The studio is made for a computer: a PC or Mac with a mouse, a keyboard and a wide screen. On a phone, Fledgetone opens **Pocket**, a simpler version made for a phone, instead (see [Fledgetone Pocket: on a phone](#fledgetone-pocket-on-a-phone)).

1. Click **START AUDIO** in the top-right corner. Browsers only allow sound after you click something, so the button glows red until audio is running. Pressing Play also starts it.
2. To hear an example, open the **File** menu at the top, choose **New…** and pick one of the eight starters (chill and world music like Lo-Fi, Celtic, Plaza and Eastern).
3. Press **Space** (or the green Play button) to play, and Space again to pause.
4. Double-click a clip to open its notes in the **piano roll** under the timeline.

> The app opens your last song again, as you left it (see Autosave). On your first visit it opens an empty song, with a button to open a starter.

At the very top is the **menu bar**: every tool in the app, where a desktop DAW keeps it (see The menu bar below), with the project's name, **Save**, **Export**, **Share** and **Game** (the Game tab, one click away) on the right. Under it is the header: **TEMPO**, **TAP** and **FIT**, **Swing**, **Intensity**, the **LISTEN** volume, the **Level-matched** mark and the loudness reading (once audio runs), **SEARCH**, **LEARN**, **GUIDE** and **START AUDIO**. Under that, top to bottom:

- **Arrangement Timeline**: the toolbar, the ruler, your tracks (in folders, if you make some), the Overview strip and the piano roll or audio editor.
- **The bottom panel**: tabs for the **Mixer**, the **Launcher** (see Clip launcher), **EQ & Dynamics**, **Game**, **Video** and **Sound FX**. Click a tab to show it; click the open tab (or the arrow on the right) to fold the panel away. Drag its top edge to make it taller or shorter (double-click the edge to reset). The tab and height are remembered in this browser.

### Tour and tutorials

The first time you open Fledgetone it offers a one-minute **tour** and nine hands-on **tutorials**. Find them again any time in the **Learn** menu at the top (every tutorial is listed there, ticked once finished), with the **LEARN** button in the header, or by searching for *tutorial*. They're listed in this order:

- **Quick tour**: points at each part of the screen and says what it's for. Just read and click **Next**.
- **Beginner: Your first song**: open a starter, play it, mute a track, swap an instrument, change the tempo, write a note, undo, and export.
- **Beginner: Make a showcase video**: File › Showcase video (YouTube, TikTok)…, tall for phones, 60 fps, your title and marks, and which tracks show.
- **Beginner: Find and keep your sounds**: search the instruments, star a favourite, save a sound to My sounds, and keep them in a file.
- **Intermediate: Shape and mix a song**: add an instrument, use the Filter, Pump and reverb, draw automation, use the Mixer, add markers, a build-up and drop, and the loop.
- **Intermediate: Write with the helpers**: a chord progression in a key, an accompaniment for it, the Drummer, melody variations, and a clip's own swing (FEEL).
- **Intermediate: Make it expressive**: a Swell from the piano roll's TOOLS, the mod wheel as dynamics, a Vibrato lane, Breathe (a tempo that eases into phrase ends) and Play backwards.
- **Intermediate: Game music that reacts**: a song with sections as states, switch states in the Game tab, the transition matrix, a written bridge, switching on the phrase, and Audition like the game.
- **Expert: Pro techniques**: search, the Synth Designer, sidechaining from any track, the Gate, key changes, freezing, the Game tab and game-ready exports, and the fastest shortcuts.
- **Expert: Make it react**: parameters the game sets, costumes, shuffled pieces and silences, effects and parameter sliders in Audition, Try it in a game, and what the game kit carries.

A tutorial puts a glowing ring round the part of the screen to use, and its card sits in a corner out of the way: it moves only if a menu, picker or dialog you open would end up under it. Drag the card by its top row to put it anywhere, or shrink it to one line with **–** (the line still shows what to do). Steps marked **Your turn** wait for you to do it and move on by themselves once you have (with a tick); everything else just needs **Next**. The app works normally the whole time, shortcuts included. **Back** goes to the previous step, **Skip** moves past a task you'd rather not do, and the **X** closes the tutorial: **LEARN** (or the **Learn** menu) then offers to **Resume** where you stopped. Finished tutorials are ticked. Progress is remembered in this browser.

### Search

Press `Ctrl+K` (`Cmd+K` on a Mac), or click **SEARCH** in the header, and type what you're after: a tool (*export*, *mixer*, *mic*), a setting (*swing*, *reverb*, *stable playback*, *count-in*), an instrument (*violin*, *8-bit*, *angelic*), a starter (*celtic*) or anything in this guide (*frame rate*). Results are labelled **Tool**, **Setting**, **Instrument**, **Starter** or **Guide**; the best match is first. A word with one letter wrong still finds it when nothing matches as typed (*viloin*). A result too long for its row shows its whole name and description when you point at it or reach it with the arrow keys.

Up and Down choose, `Enter` goes: a tool opens (Export, the Mixer...), a setting is scrolled into view and ringed in blue (opening the Inspector or the recording options first if it's in there), a guide result opens the guide at that section, and a starter shows it in the New dialog. On an instrument, `Enter` adds a track with it and `Shift+Enter` puts it on the selected track. Piano roll tools (scale, chords, arpeggiate...) need a clip open in the piano roll; search says so if none is. `Esc` closes search.

## The timeline

The timeline is 400 bars long. It starts in 4/4 at one tempo; you can add tempo and time signature changes along the ruler (see Tempo changes and time signatures). Each row is a track: its header on the left (name, volume, pan, mute, solo), its clips on the right.

### Tools

- **Selection tool** (`V`, the arrow): select, move, trim and create clips.
- **Razor tool** (`C`, the scissors): click a clip to cut it in two at that point. A red line previews the cut.

### Creating clips

- With the Selection tool, double-click empty lane space for a new 1-bar clip starting on that beat (whatever SNAP is set to). It opens in the piano roll.
- Right-click empty lane space for empty clips of 1, 2 or 4 bars, the writing helpers (**Drummer…** on a drum kit; **Chord progression…**, **Math patterns…** and **Dark melody…** on a pitched instrument: see Smart tools and Arranging a beat), ready-made patterns (every drum kit, Slap Bass, Space Clav, Brass Stabs and the EDM instruments have them), and **Paste here** or **Paste at playhead**. On an Audio track it offers **Load audio file…** instead.
- The **+** in a track header opens the same menu at the playhead.

### Selecting clips

- Click a clip to select it. **Shift**, **Ctrl** or **Cmd**-click adds or removes clips.
- Drag a box over empty lane space to select every clip it touches (hold Shift to add to the selection).
- `Ctrl+A` selects all clips. `Esc` or a click on empty space clears the selection.

### Editing clips

- **Move**: drag the middle of a clip. All selected clips move together, sideways in time and up or down to other tracks. To ignore snap, start dragging and then hold **Shift** (Shift-clicking a clip selects it instead). A click that wobbles by a few pixels changes nothing.
- **Move to another track**: a clip can only go onto a track whose instrument can play it: the same instrument, drum kit to any other drum kit, FX track to FX track, or any pitched instrument to any other (the Sampler and Taiko count as pitched). While you drag, the tracks it can land on are outlined in cyan; over any other track the clips stay on their own tracks. If several clips are selected and any one can't go to its new track, none of them change track, so a section spread over several tracks stays lined up.
- **Scroll while dragging**: bring the pointer to the edge of the track area and the timeline scrolls that way, faster the closer you get. The mouse wheel works mid-drag too.
- **Alt-drag**: hold **Alt** (Option on a Mac) and drag. Copies stay behind and the originals move.
- **Duplicate** (`Ctrl+D`, or right-click a clip > Duplicate): copies the selected clips as one block right after the end of the selection, so a section spread over several tracks stays lined up. The copies become the selection, so pressing `Ctrl+D` again keeps extending the section. If the copy wouldn't fit before bar 401, nothing is copied and a message says why.
- **Duplicate linked** (`Ctrl+Shift+D`, or right-click a clip > Duplicate linked): like Duplicate, but each copy is **linked** to its original, as a pattern is in a pattern-based DAW: edit the notes of any of them (in the piano roll, with a tool, by recording into it) and every linked clip changes with it, wherever it is. Linked clips show a badge with the pattern's name (**P1**, **P2**…). Each keeps its own place, length and track; they share their notes, controller lanes, groove and wobble. Duplicate linked again (of an original or a copy) adds more clips to the same pattern. **Make unique** (right-click, or in the piano roll) unlinks a clip: it keeps its notes, and edits stop reaching the others. Trimming a linked clip's start or splitting it makes those clips their own, since their notes move; moving, copying to the end and trimming the end don't. Edits that move notes across the whole song (a key change from a bar) change a pattern everywhere it plays. Only note clips can be linked.
- **Repeat ×N…** (right-click a clip): type how many copies you want (1 to 64) and press Enter. They go end to end after the selection, like Duplicate. If they wouldn't all fit before bar 401, the menu says how many would.
- **Fill to loop Out** (right-click a clip): repeats the selection end to end up to the loop's OUT. The last copy is cut short at OUT (its hidden notes come back if you drag its edge out). Fill makes at most 2,000 clips at a time.
- **Trim**: drag a clip's left or right edge. Trimming hides notes rather than deleting them, so dragging the edge back brings them back. Zoom in if a clip is too narrow to show its edge handles.
- **Copy, cut, paste**: `Ctrl+C`, `Ctrl+X`, `Ctrl+V`. Paste lands at the playhead (on the snap grid), on the tracks the clips came from, and selects the pasted clips. When playback is stopped, the playhead then moves to the end of the paste, so pressing `Ctrl+V` again lays copies end to end. Right-click a lane or clip for **Paste here**: if everything you copied came from one track, it pastes onto the track you right-clicked when that track's instrument can play it (the menu says "this track"); otherwise clips go back to the tracks they came from. Clips whose track no longer exists are only pasted that way.
- **Delete**: `Delete` or `Backspace`.
- Right-click a clip for **Edit notes** (**Gain and fades** on an audio clip), **Takes…** (a take folder), Copy, Cut, Paste here, Duplicate, Duplicate linked (**Make unique** on a linked clip), Repeat ×N…, **Speed up the end** (see Arranging a beat), **Play backwards** (note clips), Fill to loop Out, **Send to launcher**, **Generate variations…** (game sounds, see Micro-variations) and Delete, and what suits the clip: the writing helpers on a note clip (see Smart tools), and **Chop sample…**, **Tune vocal…**, **Hard tune…** and **Double this take** on an audio clip.
- Duplicate, Repeat, Fill and every move (time and track together) are one Undo step each.

### Snap

The **SNAP** menu in the toolbar sets the grid that clip moves, trims, razor cuts, the playhead, the loop brackets, markers and paste land on: **1/4** (a beat), **1/8**, **1/16**, **Frame** (one video frame, see Timecode & frame rates) or **Off**. Hold **Shift** to ignore snap when clicking or dragging on the ruler, razor-cutting, right-clicking or dropping a pad; for clip moves and trims, start the drag first and then hold Shift. The loop brackets always snap: set SNAP to Off to place them freely. The snap step is also the shortest a clip or the loop can be (never less than a 16th).

**Magnetic edges**: while you drag a clip or its edge (with SNAP on and Shift not held), its start or end jumps onto another clip's start or end, a marker, the playhead or a loop bracket once it comes within a few pixels, so parts butt up exactly. Further away, it snaps to the grid as usual.

### Zoom and scrolling

- Zoom with the slider at the right of the toolbar, or `+` and `-` (they keep the playhead where it is on screen when it's visible). **Ctrl+mouse wheel** (Cmd on a Mac) or a trackpad pinch zooms around the pointer. Zoom goes from 12 to 1280 pixels per bar, deep enough to see single video frames.
- Zoomed in, the lanes show faint lines on every 16th, or on every frame when SNAP is Frame.
- Scroll in time with **Shift+mouse wheel**, a sideways trackpad swipe, or the **Overview** strip under the tracks: drag its cyan window to scroll, click anywhere on it to jump, and drag either end of the window to zoom.
- **See more tracks at once**: drag the bar with the grip right under the tracks down to make the timeline taller, pushing the panel under it down (or up to make it shorter). It stops at the last track, so there's never empty space under them. Double-click it to fit every track in, and again to go back to the usual height. Adding a track grows the timeline just enough to show it (up to about three quarters of the window; past that, it scrolls to the new track). The height is remembered in this browser.
- While playing, the view follows the playhead. While stopped, a jump to a spot that's off screen (`,` and `.`, a position in the Markers list, **Go to In** or `Home`, or a position typed into a readout) scrolls the view to it.

### Undo

`Ctrl+Z` undoes and `Ctrl+Shift+Z` or `Ctrl+Y` redoes, up to 100 steps (**Edit › Undo** and **Redo** too). Undo covers:

- clip and note edits;
- Insert and Delete bars, along with where they moved the loop and the playhead;
- markers: adding, moving, renaming, locking and deleting them;
- adding and deleting tracks (Undo brings a deleted track back with its clips), and changing a track's instrument;
- M, S and R, VOL and PAN, the EQ, Distortion, Compressor, Chorus, the sends and the Master tab (OTT, Stereo Width, reverb and delay);
- loading, replacing or removing a Sampler sound, and its **Original pitch on** note;
- automation: adding, moving and deleting points, ramps and clearing or removing lanes;
- the Game tab's own parts: costumes, stingers, the game's sound effects, transition rules, and how each state plays (shuffle and silences).

Every tool that writes music for you (the smart tools, Arrange a beat, Speed up into the drop, a build-up and drop, a key change...) is one step too. One slider adjustment is one step, whether you drag it, tap its arrow keys or double-click to reset it. The starting tempo (tempo changes on the ruler are undone), swing, the loop (other than by Insert and Delete bars), zoom, snap, frame rate, ruler units, listening volume and the project name are not undone.

### Inserting and deleting bars

Right-click the time ruler. Its menu has **Add marker here** (see Markers), tempo and time signature changes (see Tempo changes), **Punch in here** and **Punch out here** (see Recording), the arranging tools below and **Cut song to length…**. Its bar edits act on **all tracks** at the point you clicked (on the snap grid; Shift for the exact point):

- **Insert 1, 2, 4 or 8 bars here**: adds empty bars. Clips that cross the point are cut in two there, and everything after it moves right. If that would push a clip or a marker past bar 401, nothing changes and the menu says so.
- **Delete bars In → Out**: removes the loop's range. Clips inside it go, clips that cross IN or OUT lose the part inside, and everything after it moves left to close the gap.

Markers, automation points, the loop and the playhead move with the music. Each edit is one Undo step, even one that only moves the loop or the playhead. Undo puts everything back, the loop and the playhead included; if either has moved since (you played, or dragged the loop), it moves back with the music instead. `Ctrl+Shift+Z` or `Ctrl+Y` does the edit again.

### Build-up & drop

Right-click the time ruler on the bar where the drop lands and choose **Build-up & drop here**. In one Undo step it writes:

- a **riser** and a **noise sweep** over the 4 bars before it, with a **reverse crash** into the drop (an FX track);
- a **snare roll** over the same 4 bars: quarters, then 8ths, then 16ths getting louder (a drum track);
- an **impact** with a **sub drop** on the drop itself, and a downlifter a bar later;
- markers called **Build** and **Drop**, unless there's already one on those bars.

It uses an FX track and a drum track that are empty there, or adds new ones (an FX track, called Build FX if the song has one already, and Snare Roll) so nothing you wrote is covered up. Near the start of the song the build is shorter (a drop at bar 3 gets 2 bars). They're ordinary clips: change, move or delete them as you like.

### Key change

Right-click the ruler where the new key starts and choose **Key change here: up a semitone**, **up a whole step** or **down a semitone**. Every pitched note from there to the end moves, in one Undo step; drum kits, percussion (Taiko and the like), sound effects, the FX track, the Slicer and audio clips stay as they are. A clip that runs across that point changes only from it on. A note that would go outside its instrument's range stays put, and the menu says how many did. The classic final-chorus lift is up a whole step, set up by a bar of the new key's V7 chord or a bar of silence.

### Cut to length: 6, 15, 30, 60 and 90-second versions

For an ad, a trailer or a social clip, the music often has to last an exact time. Right-click the time ruler and choose **Cut song to length…** (or search *cut down*), pick **6 s**, **15 s**, **30 s**, **60 s** or **90 s**, or type a length (`0:45:00` or `45`).

- It works in **sections**: the stretches between your markers (in a song without markers, 8-bar phrases; 4-bar ones in a song under 16 bars). Whole sections are left out for a shorter version, or a middle section is repeated for a longer one, so every edit falls on a bar line where the music changes anyway.
- It always **ends on the song's own ending** (its last section), keeps the sections in order, and keeps the parts that matter most: the busiest sections, and those named like a main part (Drop, Chorus, Hook, Theme, Combat, Boss...) before an Intro, Break or Build.
- The dialog shows the sections it will use, in order, with the length that makes. **Nudge the tempo** (on by default) changes the tempo by up to 8% so it lasts exactly the length you picked, to the hundredth of a second; untick it to keep your tempo and get the nearest whole number of bars.
- **Cut to** rebuilds the timeline in one Undo step: clips are cut at the section edges, automation goes with its section, the section markers move to where their sections now start (hit points locked to time stay put), and the loop covers the new version. `Ctrl+Z` brings the whole song back; a nudged tempo stays, so set it back by hand. Save a copy first if you want to keep both versions.

### Arranging a beat

Tools for rap beats and heavy 808 music, and for turning a loop into a song. Open them from **Song › Arrange a beat: drops, beat switch, song form, tag…** or **Song › Dark melody…**, right-click the ruler (**Drop, stop or beat switch here…**), right-click an empty spot on an instrument track (**Dark melody…**), or search *arrange*. Each button is one Undo step, and the results are ordinary clips, markers, automation and tempo changes, so they play the same in every export.

- **Dark melody**: a short loop (1, 2 or 4 bars) in one of four styles: **Sparse bells** (syncopated bells with space), **Sliding piano** (piano with a bounce and triplet runs), **Fast riff** (busy 16th riffs with octave jumps) or **Cowbell riff** (a stubborn 3+3+2 riff). It plays a motif, repeats it, and changes the ending (in 4 bars: the motif, an answer that hangs on a tension note, the motif, an answer that comes home), so it loops cleanly. The half step above the root (the flat second) is in every one, for that dark pull back onto the root. In **Minor**, **Harmonic minor** or **Phrygian**, in the song's key (a major song gets its relative minor), with **Density** (sparse to busy) and **Register** (low, middle, high). It goes onto the track you pick or a new **bell**, **pluck** or **piano** track. **New idea** writes another in place of the last one; the idea number is a seed, so the same number with the same settings always writes the same melody.
- **Drop out**: tick the tracks to silence (all but the melody, say) for the last half bar, 1 or 2 bars before the drop. Their notes there are cut, and a Volume automation dip silences what still rings (an 808's tail, a bell's ring).
- **Stop before the drop**: every track silent for the last beat (or half beat) of the bar before the drop, so the drop hits out of silence. It cuts the notes and writes a short Volume dip on each track (return tracks too), back to full level exactly on the drop. The master's shared reverb isn't a track, so its tail of what came before still rings faintly (very quiet, around -27 dB, but audible); turn the tracks' reverb sends down there, or use a return track for reverb, for total silence.
- **Riser** and **Reverse cymbal**: on an FX track (the FX instrument's riser and reverse crash; the sound effects maker has no riser), over 1 to 8 bars, peaking right on the drop, or a beat or half a beat early to meet a stop.
- **Beat switch**: copy bars (the loop's In to Out to start with) right after themselves with new drums and bass, the melody unchanged: **Half time** (the beat at half speed), **Double time**, or a **New drum pattern** from the Drummer (pick its style), the bass re-cut to follow the new kicks at its old pitches. The rest of the song moves later and a **Beat switch** marker names it.
- **Speed up into the drop**: the tempo rises over the end of a block of music and snaps back on the drop, the rush producers put into a build. Pick the block (**Use the loop**, **Use the selected clips**, or type the bars: it ends where the drop lands), **how much of it** speeds up (the last 10 to 100%, shown in bars and beats; it starts on a beat) and **how much faster** it gets (up to +100%, double time). **Steady** rises evenly; **Slow, then fast** creeps up and then rushes, the classic build. **At the drop** it snaps back to the old tempo (it reaches the top a 32nd before the drop) or stays at the new speed. Tick **Roll the drums up** and the snare (or clap) in the sped-up part becomes a roll, 8ths, then 16ths, then 32nds, getting louder. Quickest: **right-click a clip** (or one of several selected clips): **Speed up the end** does it at once (the last 25%, 25% faster, snapping back), **Speed up the end + drum roll** goes 50% faster, slow then fast, with the roll, and **Speed up the end…** opens these options on that block. Or right-click the ruler on the loop (or the selected clips) and choose **Speed up into here…**. It writes ordinary tempo changes (see Tempo changes), so playback, exports, the metronome, recording and the grid follow it, and the music before and after keeps its timing; tempo changes already inside the sped-up part are replaced. Audio clips that follow the tempo are cut on the beats there (more finely where it speeds up fast), each piece at its own tempo, so they keep time; audio clips that play at their own speed are left alone. One Undo puts everything back. The pitch doesn't rise with the tempo.
- **Song form**: from a 4- or 8-bar loop, the whole song: **Intro, Hook, Verse, Hook, Verse, Bridge, Hook, Outro**. Choose each section's length (none leaves it out). The intro and outro play the melody alone; verses a thinner beat (kick, snare, clap and 8th-note hats: no rolls or open hats) under a softer melody, so a voice has room; the bridge only the kick under the melody and bass; hooks everything. With the box ticked, the drums drop out for the last half bar before each hook. Markers name every section; anything after the loop moves later.
- **Producer tag**: **Record a tag** (or **Import a sound file…**) once: silence at both ends is trimmed off and it's kept in this browser for every project (clearing space with **Free unused** in the sound library doesn't delete it). **Tag the start** puts it at a bar; **Tag the whole beat** puts it every 4, 8 or 16 bars, for a beat-store preview. It goes on a **Producer tag** audio track that the rest of the beat ducks under by 6 dB (the track's **Duck others under this**), live and in exports. A project file carries the tag inside it like any other sound.

## Playback and the loop

- **Play / Pause**: the green button or `Space`. Playback starts exactly at the playhead, and notes that start right there play. With the loop on, a playhead at or past OUT jumps straight back to IN, so to play later parts turn the loop off (`L`) or move OUT.
- **Stop** (the square): stops and sends the playhead back to the loop's In point.
- **Go to In** (the skip-back button, or `Home`). If the In point is off screen, the view scrolls to it.
- **Playhead**: click or drag anywhere on the ruler, or drag the red line. Works while playing too.

### The loop

The amber region on the ruler, between the **IN** and **OUT** brackets, is the loop. With loop on (the Repeat button or `L`), playback goes round it, and the beat at IN plays on every pass. With it off, playback runs to the end of the timeline.

- Drag the IN and OUT brackets, or move the playhead and press `I` (set In) or `O` (set Out). They follow the snap grid; set SNAP to Off to place them anywhere.
- For exact values, click the small chevron (˅) right of the Loop button. **Loop range** has **In**, **Out** and **Length** boxes in the ruler's units (bars, or timecode in Time mode); each accepts bars (`17.3`) or a timecode (`00:00:12:15`), and Length takes bars.beats (`2.2`) or a timecode duration. Enter applies. Typed values don't snap. Typing an In at or after the current Out moves the whole loop there.
- A new project loops bars 1 to 5, so at first you only hear 4 bars. Press `L` to turn the loop off, or move OUT.

### Going to an exact position

Both readouts in the toolbar are also input boxes. Click one, type a position, and press **Enter** to jump the playhead there, scrolling the view to it if it's off screen (`Esc` cancels):

- **BAR.BEAT.TICK**: a bar (`17`), a bar and beat (`17.3`) or bar, beat and hundredths of a beat (`17.3.50`).
- **TIMECODE**: a timecode (see below).

## Tempo changes and time signatures

The thin top row of the ruler holds the song's tempo and time signature, and every change along the way. A faint line traces the tempo across the song, rising where it speeds up; a glide shows as a slope.

- **Add a tempo change**: double-click the top row, or right-click the ruler and choose **Tempo change here** (a jump) or **Tempo glide to here** (the tempo slides there from the change before, evenly over time, for a ritardando or a build that speeds up).
- **Breathe** (Song menu): the tempo eases back into the end of every 4-bar phrase (or 8, deeper) and picks up again on the next downbeat, as a pianist playing rubato does, with a longer slow-down into the last one. It writes ordinary tempo glides over the loop (the whole song when the loop is off), so live playback and every export follow it, and you can drag or delete them like any other. One Undo step takes it out. It won't write over a glide already in that stretch.
- **Speed up into the drop** (right-click a clip: **Speed up the end**; or Arrange a beat; or right-click the ruler on the loop: **Speed up into here…**): the tempo rises over the last part of a block and snaps back on the drop. See Arranging a beat.
- **Edit one**: click its flag. Type the **BPM** (40 to 240, to 0.01), tick **Glide from the tempo before** to make it a glide, or **Remove** it. Click the first flag to change the song's starting tempo (the same as TEMPO in the header).
- **Move one**: drag its flag along the row. It snaps to the grid (Shift for none).
- **Time signatures**: right-click the ruler and choose **Time signature change here**, or click the time signature at the start of the row for the song's own. Pick the beats per measure (1 to 16) and the beat (2, 4, 8 or 16): 3/4, 6/8, 7/8, 5/4 and so on. A change lands on the nearest measure line, and dragging its flag keeps it on measure lines.

What changes and what doesn't:

- The ruler counts **measures** in their time signature, and the grid, the bar lines in the lanes and the BAR.BEAT.TICK readout follow it. The metronome clicks each beat of the measure, accented on beat 1, and the count-in is one measure of the song's time signature.
- Clips, notes, the loop and markers stay where they are in the music when the tempo changes: they keep their place in beats, and their time in seconds moves. Timecode, the video, and the time readouts follow the tempo.
- Audio clips that follow the tempo (stretched to it) play at the tempo where they start. Audio clips that don't play at their own speed, so their length in bars follows the tempo there.
- Exports, stems, freezing and every game export follow the tempo map to the sample, and so does playback, the loop included (a loop that crosses a change goes back to the tempo at its In each time round).
- Inserting and deleting bars moves the changes after them (deleting bars deletes the changes in them). Every change is one Undo step.

## Timecode and frame rates

The **TIMECODE** readout shows the playhead as hours, minutes, seconds and frames (`HH:MM:SS:FF`), counted from the start of bar 1 at the song's tempo. Use it to line music up with video or a game.

### Choosing a frame rate

Click the frame rate next to **TIMECODE** (it starts at **30 FPS**) and pick one:

| Rate | Typical use |
|---|---|
| 23.976 fps | Film shot for NTSC video ("24p" on most cameras) |
| 24 fps | Film and animation at a true 24 fps |
| 25 fps | PAL video (Europe, Australia) |
| 29.97 fps | NTSC video, non-drop-frame |
| 29.97 DF | NTSC broadcast video, drop-frame |
| 30 fps | Games, screen recordings and web video at a true 30 fps |
| 60 fps | Games and high-frame-rate video |

The frame rate is saved with the project. Projects from before this setting open at 30 fps. **New** keeps the frame rate you're using.

- **23.976** and **29.97** count 24 or 30 frames a second, but each frame lasts 1001/1000 as long, matching camera and broadcast footage. Non-drop 29.97 runs 3.6 seconds an hour behind the clock, as in video editors.
- **29.97 DF** (drop-frame) is written with a `;` before the frames (`00:01:00;02`). It stays in step with the clock by skipping the frame numbers `;00` and `;01` at the start of every minute except every tenth. Typing a skipped label goes to the next real frame.

### Typing a timecode

Click the timecode, type, and press Enter:

- `00:01:12:10` is 1 minute, 12 seconds, frame 10. Fields fill in from the right (frames, then seconds, minutes, hours), so you can leave off leading zeros: `1:12:10` is the same place, and `12:10` is 12 seconds, frame 10. Colons, semicolons, dots or spaces all separate fields.
- Digits on their own fill in from the frames, like in video editors: `1500` is 15 seconds, `10215` is 1 minute 2 seconds and 15 frames, and `12` is frame 12.
- Fields can overflow: `90:00` is 90 seconds.
- In drop-frame, `;` before the frames is optional.

A red box means the entry couldn't be read, or is past the end of the timeline.

### A timecode ruler

The **Bars | Time** switch above the track names changes what the ruler counts. **Time** labels it as `M:SS:FF` (minutes, seconds, frames), so any label can be typed straight into the timecode box. Labels come every few seconds when zoomed out, and every few frames when zoomed in, with smaller ticks in between. In drop-frame, minute labels read like `1:00;02`.

### Snapping to frames

Set **SNAP** to **Frame** to make clip moves, clip edges, razor cuts, the playhead, the loop brackets and markers land exactly on video frames at the current frame rate. (A clip made by double-clicking a lane still starts on a beat; drag it to put it on a frame.) The Export dialog also shows the loop's start timecode, so you know where a loop export sits against the picture.

> For frame-accurate work, **set the tempo and frame rate first**: positions are stored in bars, so changing either one leaves things off the new frame grid. Also set **Swing** to 0%, because swing delays the off-beat 16th notes slightly.

Frame snap applies to the timeline. Notes you write in the piano roll sit on its own grid (1/16, 1/8 or 1/4), counted from the clip's start. A pad dropped into a clip with Frame snap on lands on a frame instead; the piano roll shows it at the nearest 16th, and moving or nudging it puts it back on the grid.

## Scoring to a video

Write music to a gameplay clip, a trailer or a cutscene, and see the picture while you work.

- Click **Video** in the **Video** tab of the bottom panel (or **Video › Load reference video…**, or drop a video file anywhere on the page) to load an MP4 or WebM. It appears as a **video lane** under the ruler, with its frames along the song at the current tempo, and in a **preview window** that follows the playhead: it plays along when you play, and shows the frame under the playhead when you click or drag on the ruler, so you can line hits up with the picture.
- **Drag the video lane** sideways to move where the video starts in the song. It snaps to beats; hold Shift to place it freely. The lane's header shows where it starts (time and bar).
- **Find the cuts**: the scissors on the video lane's header looks through the video for hard cuts and drops a hit point (a marker locked to time) on each one, named **Cut 1**, **Cut 2**..., at the frame where the new shot starts. It takes a second or two for a short clip (the header shows how far it's got; click it again to stop). Cuts already marked aren't added twice. Then **FIT › Hit points** finds a tempo that lands the music on them. Move the video first: the markers go where the cuts fall in the song now.
- **Dip under dialogue**: the speech bubble on the video lane's header listens to the video's own sound for voices and shows how much talking it found. Pick how far the music should dip (**-3** to **-24 dB**, -10 to start) and click **Dip the music**: every track's volume dips from a moment before each line until half a second after it, with short pauses between words bridged so the music doesn't bob up mid-sentence. It's written as **volume automation** (one Undo step), so you can see each dip on the tracks, move or reshape it, and it's in every export. A track that already has a volume lane keeps its shape, lowered in the dips; remember a volume lane takes over from the track's fader. It listens for anything loud in the voice range, so loud effects or music in the video can count too: check the dips and delete any you don't want. A video with no sound the browser can read says so ("Couldn't listen to the video") instead.
- The preview window: drag its top bar to move it, the arrows button cycles its size (small, medium, large), the speaker button lets you hear the video's own sound while you work (off by default), and **×** hides it. The eye button on the video lane shows it again; **×** on the lane removes the video.
- To export the music **on** your video, choose **Video (MP4)** in Export. With a video loaded, **Put the music on** *your video* is ticked: the picture is your video, where it sits on the timeline (black before and after), instead of the visualizer. Tick **Keep the video's own sound too** to mix its sound effects and dialogue under the music.
- The **Video** tab of the bottom panel has it all in one place: load or remove the video, its preview and sound, where it starts (type a time), **Find cuts** and **Dip under dialogue**, the hit points and the **Tempo finder**, **Cut to length**, the markers for **Premiere / Resolve / Final Cut**, and exporting the music onto the video.
- **The video is saved with the project.** It's kept in this browser, so a reload (or **Restore** of a replaced song) brings it back, where it was. A saved project file carries the video inside it when it's 40 MB or less; a bigger one stays in this browser, and opening the project elsewhere says which video it scores to (load it again and it goes back to its place).

## Markers

Markers are named points on the timeline: the sections of a song (intro, build, drop), or hit points in a video.

### Adding markers and jumping to them

- Press `M` to drop a marker at the playhead, even while playing. Markers are named **Marker 1**, **Marker 2** and so on. If a marker is already at that exact spot, nothing is added and a message says so.
- Press `Shift+M` to drop a **hit point** instead: a marker named **Hit 1**, **Hit 2**... that's locked to time, for a moment in a video the music should land on (a door slam, a cut, a logo). The **tempo finder** uses them (below).
- Or right-click the time ruler and choose **Add marker here**: it goes where you clicked, on the snap grid (hold Shift for the exact point).
- Or open the **Markers** list and click **At playhead**.
- `,` (comma) jumps to the previous marker and `.` (period) to the next. While playing, `,` skips a marker you passed less than half a second ago, so pressing it again and again keeps going back. Holding the key down while playing jumps once; holding it while stopped steps through the markers one by one. If the marker is off screen, the view scrolls to it.

### Flags on the ruler

Each marker is a violet flag with its name in the ruler, just under the numbers, with a thin violet line down through the tracks. The lines don't get in the way: clips, box selection and automation work straight through them. A long name is cut short to fit before the next flag; hover a flag to see its full name, position and timecode.

- **Click** a flag to move the playhead there.
- **Drag** it to move the marker. It snaps to the SNAP grid; hold Shift for no snap.
- **Double-click** it to rename it (up to 80 characters). Enter or a click anywhere else keeps the name; `Esc` cancels.
- **Right-click** it for **Rename**, **Lock to time** (or **Lock to bar**) and **Delete**. Unlike a click, the menu leaves the playhead where it is, so it doesn't interrupt playback.
- **With the keyboard**: `Tab` reaches each flag. `Enter` moves the playhead there, `F2` renames it, `Delete` deletes it, `←` / `→` move it one SNAP step (with Shift, a bar), and the menu key or `Shift+F10` opens its menu.

A marker normally stays on its bar when you change the tempo, so it moves with the music. **Lock to time** keeps its timecode instead and lets its bar move, for lining up with a video; its flag shows a small lock. If a faster tempo pushes a locked marker past the end of the timeline, it isn't drawn and the list says "after the end"; it comes back when you slow the tempo down.

### The tempo finder: land the music on your hit points

Drop hit points on the moments in a video that matter (`Shift+M`, or **Lock to time** on any marker), then click **FIT** next to the tempo and choose **Hit points**. It tries every tempo in the range (70 to 180 BPM unless you type another, in steps of 0.01) and lists the five that put the most hits on the grid:

- **Bar**, **Beat** or **1/8**: what the hits should land on. A hit counts when it's within one frame at your frame rate.
- Each suggestion shows how many hits it lands and how far off they are on average; click it to see every hit's timecode, the bar and beat it lands on, and how early or late it is.
- **Apply** sets that tempo. Hit points keep their times, so they now sit on the grid, ready for a crash or a stab right on them. The tempos assume one steady tempo: a song with tempo changes gets a note saying so, since Apply sets the tempo from bar 1 and hits after a change can then miss (remove the changes first).
- The line above the list says how many land at the tempo you have now.

### The Markers list

Click the flag button right of the Loop button (it shows how many markers there are). The list has every marker in order, with its position (bar.beat.hundredths) and its timecode at your frame rate, drop-frame included. It grows to fill the room under it, and scrolls when there are more markers than fit.

- Type over a name to rename the marker; the flag changes as you type.
- The lock button locks the marker to time, or back to its bar. The bin deletes it.
- Click a position to move the playhead there (the view scrolls to it if it's off screen).
- `Esc` or a click outside closes the list.
- **Export markers for…** saves every marker (sections and hit points) as a file your video editing software imports, so the editor sees the same names at the same moments on their timeline:
  - **Premiere Pro (.xml)**: File › Import; it opens as a sequence carrying the markers.
  - **DaVinci Resolve (.edl)**: right-click the timeline in the Media Pool, or Timeline › Import › Timeline Markers from EDL. Resolve timelines usually start at 01:00:00:00, so tick **Timeline starts at 01:00:00:00** first.
  - **Final Cut Pro (.fcpxml)**: File › Import › XML; a project with the markers on its timeline.
  - **Spreadsheet (.csv)**: name, timecode and seconds.

  They use your frame rate (drop-frame included). With a reference video loaded, **From the video's start** (on) counts from the video's first frame, where it sits on the editor's timeline; untick it to count from bar 1.

### Markers and the rest of the song

- Adding, moving, renaming, locking and deleting a marker are one Undo step each.
- **Insert bars** moves the markers at or after the insert point with the music. **Delete bars In → Out** deletes the markers in the removed bars (one exactly at IN goes, one exactly at OUT is kept) and moves the later ones left. Markers locked to time stay where they are.
- Markers are saved with the project and in autosave, even before there are any clips (hit points placed on a video before the music is written): the Restore banner offers that session too. Older versions of the app open the file without them.
- WAV exports carry the markers as cue points (see Exporting audio).

## Writing notes: the piano roll

Double-click a clip with the Selection tool (or right-click it and choose **Edit notes**, which works with either tool) to open it in the piano roll under the timeline. Drum kits show a row per drum (Kick, Snare, Clap, the hats, toms, crash, ride and cowbell); other instruments show a keyboard covering the instrument's range.

- **Add a note**: click empty grid. Keep the button down and drag right to stretch it, to the grid line past the pointer.
- **Move**: drag a note sideways (time) or up and down (pitch). Selected notes move together.
- **Length**: drag a note's right edge.
- **Delete**: right-click a note, or select notes and press `Delete`.
- **Select**: click a note. Shift, Ctrl or Cmd-click adds notes; `Ctrl+A` selects them all; `Esc` clears the selection.
- **Nudge**: arrow keys move selected notes by one grid step or one semitone; Shift with up and down moves an octave.
- **Hear it**: click the keys on the left. Adding or clicking a note plays it, and so does dragging it to a new pitch.

The header has the clip's name (click to rename), **GRID** (1/32, 1/16T, 1/16, 1/8T, 1/8, 1/4), **NEW NOTE** length (it starts at the length you last drew, stretched or picked for that kind of part in this session: a quarter note for pads and chords, an eighth for other notes, a 16th for drums, a bar for FX; Pocket's piano roll shares it), **VEL** (velocity: how hard new notes are played; with notes selected it changes those notes), **PLAYS** (how often the selected notes play: see below), **FEEL** (this clip's own swing, in place of the song's) and zoom. Click the piano roll's ruler to move the playhead. Close it with **Close** or `Esc` (when no notes are selected).

**Fine grids and triplets.** **T** means triplet: three in the space of two, so 1/16T puts six notes in a beat and 1/8T three. Clicking places a note on the grid line at or before the pointer. Dragging a note, or its right edge, snaps it to the grid, and the arrow keys move notes one grid step. Changing the grid never moves existing notes. Zoom in (the magnifier) for comfortable 1/32 editing. Swing leaves triplets and 1/32 notes on the grid, and an 8th with a 1/32 roll or a triplet in it plays straight, so rolls stay even with Swing on.

**Step sequencer** (drum clips): with **STEPS** ticked (the default for drum kits), a click puts a hit in that GRID step of the row, and a click on a hit takes it out, like a drum machine. Shift-click adds an accent (full velocity). Alt-click (or STEPS off) adds, selects and drags notes as usual.

**Notes that don't play every time** (**PLAYS**, with notes selected), as on a drum machine's step conditions, so a repeated section keeps changing a little without writing it out again. **75%**, **50%** or **25% of the time**: each time round, the note plays that often. **1st of every 2**, **2nd of every 2**, **1st of every 4**, **4th of every 4**: the note plays only on that pass (an open hat on the 4th of 4 lands every fourth time; 1st of 2 and 2nd of 2 on two hats swap them over). **Always** puts the note back to normal. Such notes are drawn fainter, dashed and marked (**50%**, **1:2**), and fainter on the timeline too.

What a pass is: the copies of a clip side by side on its track (linked copies, Duplicate, Pocket's section copies, the launcher's repeats) count 1, 2, 3...; a different clip in between starts the count again, so in A A B A the last A is on its first pass. With the loop on, each time round the loop counts on as if the loop were written out as copies (a loop over one clip: pass 1, 2, 3...). It's not random as it plays: whether a note plays on a pass is worked out from the note, its clip and the pass, so the song sounds the same every time you play it from the start, and the WAV export sounds like the first time round. An export plays the arrangement once; a seamless loop export plays its loop as the first pass every time, so the file loops cleanly. Each copy of a clip makes its own 50% choices.

**Roll beat** (drum clips) rolls the beat again in the style it sounds most like: the kick, snare and clap (and the crashes and tom fills) stay, the hats, rides and percussion are new, with a soft extra here and there that often plays only half the time. Press it again for another roll; each is one Undo step. **Roll the beat again** in a drum clip's right-click menu does the same.

**Composing helpers** (in the piano roll's header):

- **SCALE**: pick a key (root and scale: Major, Minor, Harmonic minor, Dorian, Phrygian, Lydian, Mixolydian, the two pentatonics, Blues, Japanese, and the colours game music leans on: **Hirajoshi** and **Yo** (Japanese), **Phrygian dominant** and **Double harmonic** (desert), **Hungarian minor** (gothic), **Harmonic major**, **Melodic minor**, **Lydian dominant**, **Locrian**, **Whole tone** (dreams, magic), the two **Diminished** scales (menace, horror) and **Chromatic**). The CHORD stamps also have Min6, Min(maj7), 6/9, Dim7, Augmented and a 3-semitone Cluster; on the Chromatic scale, "Triad in key" stacks semitones, a cluster too. Rows outside the key are shaded dark and the root's rows lit, and a click lands on the nearest note of the key, so nothing sounds wrong.
- **CHORD**: a click adds a whole chord from the note you click. **Triad in key** and **7th chord in key** build the right chord for that place in the scale (in A minor, a click on D gives D minor); the others are fixed shapes (Major, Minor, Sus2, Sus4, 7, Maj7, Min7, Add9, Diminished, Power, Octave). The chord drags as one.
- **TOOLS** changes the selected notes, or the whole clip with nothing selected, in one Undo step: **Quantize** (starts, or starts and lengths, to the GRID), **Humanize** (small random shifts in timing and velocity), **Strum** (a chord's notes a moment apart, low to high or high to low), **Arpeggiate** (each chord becomes a broken chord at the GRID's speed: up, down, up and down, or random), **Legato** (each note lasts until the next: on the one-note-at-a-time synths they then glide into each other, and a repeated pitch holds on as one note), **Ratchet x2 / x3 / x4** (each note split into equal repeats, getting louder), **Roll** (each note repeated at the GRID's speed for its length, rising: set GRID to 1/32 for a snare or hat roll), **Semitone up / down**, **Octave up / down**, **Fit to scale**, and the tools under Swells, bends and playing backwards. Drum clips have Quantize, Humanize, Ratchet, Roll, Reverse notes and Stutter build.
- **ROLL…** (the hat roll painter) turns the selected notes, or every note with none selected, into rolls: each note becomes a roll over its own length, so select one hat (Alt-click it in STEPS) or draw one long note over the span you want rolled. **Rate**: 1/16, 1/16 triplet, 1/32, 1/32 triplet, 1/64, **Stutter** (2 to 16 hits spread evenly over each note, whatever its length), or **Accelerating** (the snare build into a drop: 8ths, then 16ths, 32nds and 64ths, a quarter of the note each; a note too short for the 8ths starts on 16ths). **Velocity**: rising (into the next bar), falling, swell (up and back down) or even. **Pitch**: even, rising (+2, +3, +5, +7 or +12 semitones over the roll, the climbing hats of rap beats) or falling. On a drum kit the pitch is each hit's own (every kit plays it); on other instruments the notes move by semitones. The hits sit exactly on the rate's grid from the note's start (a triplet roll on the triplet grid), and swing leaves an 8th with a roll in it even. One Undo step.
- **GROOVE…** (drum clips): swing per drum. Pick a template, **Straight**, **Light swing (hats)**, **Heavy swing (hats)**, **Lazy** (snare and clap a hair late, the hats swung and late) or **Pushed** (the hats rush ahead of the beat, off-beats accented), or set each row in use on its own: its **Swing** (0 to 75%, its own in place of the clip's), **Timing** (early or late by up to a quarter of a 16th), **Velocity** and **Off-beats** (the velocity of its notes between the 8ths). The templates keep the kick, and but for Lazy the snare, straight and on time, so the hats swing over a straight kick and snare. A row's swing counts only its own notes, so a hat roll keeps just that 8th of the hats even. It plays the same live, in exports, frozen and in the game kit, and FEEL then reads **Per-row groove**.
- **GHOSTS** (pitched clips) shows the other tracks' notes over the same bars as faint outlines in their colours, so a melody can follow the chords.

**Chords, arpeggios and riffs** (the row under the piano roll's header, on pitched clips):

- **IN (key) I ii iii IV V vi vii°**: the seven chords of the key, named as musicians name them (upper case major, lower case minor, ° diminished; in a minor key i ii° III iv v VI VII). The key is the **SCALE** when it's set to a seven-note scale, else the song's key as heard. Click one to add that chord at the playhead, **NEW NOTE** long: the playhead then moves on by that much, so clicking **I**, **V**, **vi**, **IV** writes a whole progression, each chord voiced close to the one before as a keyboard player would. Hover a button for the chord's name; **7THS** makes them four-note 7th chords.
- **ARP**: turns each chord (notes starting together) into an arpeggio for as long as it lasts: **Up**, **Down**, **Up and down**, **Down and up**, **Converge** (outside in), **Pinky** (the top note between the others) or **Thumb** (the bottom note between), **Random**; over 1, 2 or 3 octaves; at the GRID's speed or 1/8, 1/8T, 1/16, 1/16T, 1/32; and **staccato** to **legato**. **Apply** changes the selected chords, or every chord in the clip with none selected. (The four quick arpeggios in **TOOLS** are still there.)
- **RIFF**: **New riff** writes a riff in the key over the whole clip, replacing its notes: a **Pluck riff** (short, syncopated 16ths), a **Lead line** (longer notes that sing) or a **Bass line** (low, on the chord roots with passing notes). It's a one-bar rhythm played as a call and an answer, with its strong beats on the chords your other tracks play there (the track with the most chords over the clip), within the instrument's range. Press it again for another riff; each press is one Undo step, and the same clip gives the same riffs in the same order.

**Expression lanes** (at the bottom of the piano roll, for every instrument except the drum kits and FX): what a keyboard's wheels, pedal and knobs would play over the clip, and each note's own expression. Pick the lane in the menu at the left of the lane.

- **Pitch bend**: every note bends together. Click to add a point, drag one to move it (it snaps to the grid; Shift for none), and right-click or double-click a point to remove it; the bend glides from point to point. The lane shows ±2 semitones; **±2 st** switches the view to ±12, then ±24 (the Note bend's full range). Bends reach up to an octave either way.
- **Mod wheel (dynamics)**: how loud and how bright, from soft (bottom) to full (top), on every instrument, as orchestral sample libraries use it. Instruments recorded soft and loud and held (the **Violins**, **Violas**, **Cellos**, **Tremolo**, **Solo Violin**, **Violin**, **Viola**, **Cello**, **Contrabass** and **Double Bass**, **Oboe**, **Clarinet**, **Bassoon**, **French Horn**, **Trumpet**, **Trombone**, **Tuba** and the saxes, all **(Real)**) crossfade from the soft recording to the loud one while the note plays; every other instrument gets louder (about 18 dB from bottom to top) and brighter. A held note can swell and fade: draw a rising line for a crescendo, or let **Swell** draw it (below). A clip without a mod wheel lane plays as it always did, each note's velocity setting its loudness. A MIDI keyboard's wheel does the same live, and is recorded into this lane.
- **Vibrato**: vibrato, deeper as it goes up (at the top, half a semitone either way, about five and a half times a second). Drawn like the bend. (In songs saved before the mod wheel became dynamics, the mod wheel lane was vibrato: opening one moves it to this lane, so it sounds as it did.)
- **Sustain pedal**: drag along the lane to hold the pedal down there; notes let go while it's down ring on until it comes up. Click a held stretch to lift it.

- **Controller lanes (CC)**: any MIDI controller by number (choose one under **Add a controller lane**). Drawn like the bend, from 0 at the bottom to 127 at the top. A controller lane moves the track setting it's mapped to with MIDI learn, or else the usual one: **CC 7** volume, **11** expression (also volume: a string or wind player's swell within a phrase), **10** pan, **74** filter cutoff, **71** resonance, **91** reverb send, **93** delay send. Plugin instruments also get it as MIDI. Knobs on your controller that aren't mapped are recorded into controller lanes.
- **Note bend** and **Note pressure** (select one note): that note's own expression, as an MPE keyboard plays it. **Note bend** slides just that note away from its pitch and back (up to 24 semitones either way); **Note pressure** swells and fades it (its level, from silent at the bottom to full at the top). The lane covers the note's length. The Multi Sampler plays these on each note on its own; other instruments bend as a whole while the note plays.

A ● in the lane menu means that lane has something in it. The lanes travel with the clip when it's moved, copied, split or trimmed, and play the same live and in exports. Bends and vibrato play on the recorded instruments, Slap Bass, Sub-Bass, 808 Bass, the Wobble, Growl and Reese basses, Analog Lead, Space Clav, Brass Stabs, Wavetable Synth, the Synth Designer and plugin instruments; the sustain pedal works on every instrument.

**Notation**: tick **NOTATION** in the piano roll's header to see the clip as sheet music: a staff (a grand staff when it spans both clefs; drum clips on a percussion staff) in the song's key and meter, with chords, rests, ties over bar lines, beams and accidentals. Notes are put on a 16th grid and each staff reads as one voice (notes that start together are a chord, held until the next one starts). The measure the playhead is in is lit. **MusicXML** saves the clip for notation programs (MuseScore, Sibelius, Dorico and others), and **Print** prints it or saves it as a PDF. Untick NOTATION to edit the notes again.

### Swells, bends and playing backwards

Tools that shape a performance. All but Play backwards are in the piano roll's **TOOLS** menu, and each is one Undo step.

- **Swell, Crescendo, Diminuendo**: the mod wheel drawn for you over the selected notes (all of the clip's with none selected). Notes that touch or overlap are one phrase, and each phrase grows to its middle and fades (Swell), grows (Crescendo) or fades (Diminuendo). The mod wheel lane opens to show it; edit the points as you like, and one Undo takes it out.
- **Stutter build, Spring bounce, Tritone slide, Scoop**: **Stutter build** retriggers whatever is on each bar's last beat 2, 4, 8, then 16 times, bar by bar, tightening into the next phrase (on drums too). **Spring bounce** draws each note's own bend so its pitch boings like a weight on a spring and settles (an 808 favourite). **Tritone slide** holds each note, then slides it up half an octave (the "devil's interval"). **Scoop** starts each note a little flat and swoops up into it, the rave hoover's move. They're drawn as each note's **Note bend**, so you can reshape them.
- **Shape dynamics**: makes a line's loudness rise and fall the way a player phrases it, without drawing anything. The notes are split into phrases at each rest (or every two bars); in each, the loudness grows to the phrase's highest note and falls away after it, higher notes leaning a little louder and the last note easing off. Each phrase keeps its average, so the part's level and its accents between phrases stay as you wrote them. It changes the velocities, once (Undo to take it back).
- **Play backwards**: a block of music turned round, as a tape played backwards: notes swell in from nothing and their echoes and reverb come before them. **Song › Play bars … backwards (In → Out)** does everything between the loop's In and Out; right-click a clip of notes and choose **Play backwards** for that one clip, through its own track. The music is rendered (instruments, inserts, sends and reverb, the master section left for the way back) and reversed, and comes back as an audio clip on a new track in the same place, while the notes it came from leave that stretch (clips crossing its edges are cut there and keep their outside parts). One Undo brings the notes back; the audio clip's own **Reverse** button (in its audio editor) plays it forwards again. **Reverse notes** in TOOLS is the composer's kind instead: the notes stay notes, mirrored in time, the last first.

### Step lanes: the sound changing on every step

Modern bass music doesn't wobble at one speed: its bass changes colour on nearly every 16th (open, closed, growling, gritty), in a pattern that repeats with small changes. **Step lanes** write that. Click **Step lanes** in the piano roll's header, then **+ Lane**: a row of steps appears under the notes, each step holding a value for one of the track's settings, from the bottom (0%) to the top (100%).

- **What it moves** (the menu at the left of the lane's tools): on a Fledge Synth track its **macros**, named for what they do (on Bass/Growl, **Open**, **Growl** and **Grit**), and any other of its settings by name; on a plugin, its settings; on every track the **Filter** cutoff and resonance (drawing switches the Filter on), the inserts' settings (Distortion drive, Chorus, Lo-fi, Delay, Reverb, Gate), volume, pan, EQ and the sends to the reverb and delay. A clip can have up to eight lanes.
- **Drawing**: drag across the steps to set their heights (a quick drag sets every step it crosses, in a line). Right-drag (or Alt-drag) clears steps: a cleared step **holds the value before it**, so you only set the steps where the sound should change. The step length is **1/16** to start; **1/32**, **1/16T**, **1/8T** and **1/8** are in the menu next to it (changing it keeps the values where the new steps start).
- **Tools**: **Random** sets every step to a random height within the range beside it (**20–90** to start), **Some** only about half of them (the others hold); each press gives another set, and Undo the last one. **Repeat** copies the first 2 to 32 steps across the whole clip (draw one bar, repeat it, then change a few steps of the second for a variation). **←** and **→** shift the steps round by one. **Clear** empties the lane, the bin removes it.
- **Per note**: the value changes at each note that starts in a set step (and holds until the next), not at the step's start, so the sound changes with the notes: a note late in a step still starts with its own colour.
- Every change glides over 3 ms, so the steps never click, and arrives exactly where the step (or the note) starts.

The lanes belong to the clip: they move, copy, split and trim with it, linked clips share them, and they're saved in the song, its autosave and share links. Each drag or tool is one Undo step. Within a clip a step lane takes over its setting from the track's automation lane (and a controller lane) for that setting; outside the clip the track's lane plays again, or with none the setting's own value (a Fledge Synth macro's knob). While a step lane moves a plugin's setting, its knob shows the lane, as with automation. Live playback and exports play the same: the track's own settings change to the sample, and a plugin's settings within 16 samples (a third of a millisecond) of where the step starts.

**Slides and dives**: **Alt-drag** a note up or down to make it slide to that pitch by its end (a row per semitone, up to two octaves); hold **Shift** too to start the slide part-way along the note, where the pointer is. A yellow line from the note shows where it goes. Select notes and click **Dive** (or **TOOLS › Dive**) for the classic pitch dive: each starts an octave up and falls to its pitch over a 16th. **TOOLS › Remove slides and dives** takes them off. Both are drawn as each note's own **Note bend**, so you can reshape them there. Fledge Synth, the Sub-Bass, 808 Bass, Slap Bass, the Wobble, Growl and Reese basses, Analog Lead, the Synth Designer, the pads and keys and the recorded instruments play them (Fledge Synth's **Bend range** opens to 24 semitones the first time a note bends further than it reaches, and its knob then shows it as held); on an instrument that doesn't bend the line is dashed and the notes play at their own pitch, and the piano roll says so.

### Smart tools: chords, bass lines, a drummer and fills

Right-click to have parts written for you. Each one is a single Undo step, and the result is ordinary notes you can edit.

- **Chord progression…** (right-click an empty spot on an instrument track): four well-worn chords, such as I–V–vi–IV (pop) or i–VI–III–VII (epic), and the borrowed and chromatic ones game and film music lean on: ♭VI–♭VII–I (**level clear**, victory), I–♭VII–IV–I (**open field**), I–♭VI (**wonder**), I–III–IV (**hero's journey**), I–II (**magic**), I–IV–iv–I (**wistful ache**), a **calm maj7 vamp**, a **rousing march**; in minor, i–IV (**ancient**), i–♭II (**menace**), i–V–VI–V (**castle**), i–♭vi (**tragic grandeur**), i–♯iii (**sudden shock**), the **noir** line cliché, i–♭VI–♭VII–I and i–iv–V–I (**triumph**, dark to light) and vii°7–V7 (**dread**). Then the extended colours of city-night, ballad, lo-fi and funk writing: Imaj7–iii7–vi9–IVmaj7 (**city at night**), IVmaj7–iv6–Imaj7 (**bittersweet turn**), IVmaj7–V7–iii7–vi7 (**royal road**), IVmaj7–III7–vi7–I7 (**sunset**), ii9–V13sus–Imaj9 (**lo-fi, neo-soul**), IVmaj7–V7sus–iii7–vi9 (**night drive**) and, in minor, i9–IV9 (**Dorian funk vamp**), iiø7–V7–i7 (**minor turnaround**) and i–v–♭VI–♭III (**requiem**). Chord names show the colour: Cadd9, Fmaj9, Am9, Dm11, G7sus4, G13sus4. All in the song's key (worked out from its notes; pick another key in the dialog). Choose how long each chord lasts, how many times through, and how they're played: **Held chords**, a **Quarter-note pulse**, **Off-beat stabs** or an **Arpeggio**. **7th chords** makes them jazzier. Each chord is voiced to move as little as it can from the last, as a keyboard player would. The clip is named after its chords and starts on the bar line you clicked.
- **Math patterns…** (right-click an empty spot on an instrument track): a clip a formula writes, in the song's key (or one you pick), 4, 8 or 16 bars long. **Pendulum wave**: six notes swinging at slightly different speeds (6 to 11 swings every 4 bars), each playing as it crosses the middle; they drift apart into ripples and line up again every 4 bars (bells, a celesta or a harp suit it). **Fibonacci groove**: the Fibonacci numbers, counted in fours, pick tones of the home chord; the pattern repeats every 6 notes against 8 in a bar, so each bar lands differently (a pluck or bass). **Fifths walk**: every fourth note of the scale, a line that walks the circle of fifths through the key. **Pascal's harp**: Pascal's triangle with only its odd numbers kept (a Sierpinski fractal), one row a step, played as a rising harp chord. **Endless riser**: copies of a rising line an octave apart, each fading in at the bottom and out at the top, so it seems to rise forever and never gets higher (a Shepard tone): for builds and tension.
- **Chords for this melody** (right-click a note clip): chords that fit the clip's tune, a chord a bar (half a bar for a clip of two bars or less), on a new track under it. Long notes and notes on the beat count most; the chords are chosen to move the way songs usually do, ending on the home chord or the V.
- **Bass line from these chords** (right-click a chord clip): a driving 8th-note bass on each chord's root, on a new Slap Bass track under it (change the instrument as you like).
- **Melody variations…** (right-click a note clip): the tune another way, in the song's key (shown and changeable: a major key and its relative minor share their notes, so pick the one the tune feels like). Every note moves by the scale's own steps, so it stays in key; a sharp or flat outside the scale keeps it. **Answer phrase** (the same rhythm going the other way, starting a step from where the tune ended and coming home to the key note: call and response), **Darker** (the minor; a minor tune goes Phrygian), **Brighter** (the major; a major tune goes Lydian), **Simpler** (only the notes on the beat, each held to the next), **Busier** (passing notes filling the leaps), **Syncopated** (downbeat notes an eighth early), **Up a step** (a sequence that builds), **Mirror** (upside down around the first note), **Harmony a third above** (a second voice to play along), or **Surprise me** (two of them together; make another for another). **Put it** after the clip on its track (the first free spot), on a new track under it (the harmony's default, to play together), or in place of it. One undo step.
- **Accompaniment for these chords…** (right-click a chord clip): a part that follows each chord change, on a new track under it: a **Walking bass** (quarters up the chord, a half step into the next one), **Oom-pah-pah** (the root low on the beat, the chord on the others: a waltz in 3/4, an oom-pah in 4/4, a lilt in 6/8), an **Alberti bass**, **Strummed guitar**, a **Harp glissando** on each change, a driving **String ostinato** accented 3+3+2, **Sparkle** (a few high chord notes here and there), a **Horn fanfare** (a triplet pickup, then up the chord) or a **Drone**. Each suggests an instrument; pick another if you like. The notes are moved into the instrument's range.
- **Drummer…** (right-click an empty spot on a drum-kit track): a beat in one of twenty-two styles (**Four on the floor**, **Backbeat**, **Boom bap**, **Rap beat**, **Two-step**, **Funk**, **Dembow**, **Half time**, **Syncopated drop** (modern bass music around 100 BPM: about six kicks a bar, one to three 16ths apart, the snare on two and four, 16th hats leaning on each beat with 32nd or triplet rolls now and then), and for games: **Waltz** in 3/4, **Jig** in 6/8, **March**, **Shuffle**, **Big-band swing**, **Bossa nova**, **Calypso**, **Maqsum** desert hand drums, **Battle ostinato**, **Tribal build**, **Blast beat**, a **Heartbeat**, and **Brushes**: a soft swirl on the snare leaning on two and four, for ballads and a city at night), 1 to 16 bars (measures, in 3/4 or 6/8) long. The beats follow the song's time signature; a waltz or a jig in a 4/4 song offers to set 3/4 or 6/8 for the clip (and back after it), in the same Undo step. The shuffle and swing styles give their clip swung 8ths (see FEEL). An **Energy** slider goes from sparse through steady to busy, and **A fill every** 2, 4 or 8 bars (or none) adds fills. A crash opens the part. Every clip it makes is a new take: the hats breathe and the kicks move a little from bar to bar.
- **Roll the beat again** (right-click a drum clip): new hats and percussion in the style the beat sounds like, the same kick and snare (see Notes that don't play every time, under the piano roll).
- **Fill** (right-click a drum clip): replaces the end of the bar under the pointer with a **snare roll** (last beat), a **tom run** (last two beats), a **build-up** (the whole bar, snare 8ths to 16ths to 32nds, getting louder) or a **kick pickup**. Kicks on the beat stay, and a crash and kick land on the next downbeat.

> With no notes selected, `Delete` acts on the timeline and deletes the selected clip, usually the one you're editing. `Ctrl+Z` brings it back.

### Real recordings

The instruments marked **(Real)** are proper multi-sampled recordings, made the way commercial sample libraries are, rather than the general-purpose (General MIDI) soundfont sounds of the 1990s many of the others use:

- **Several loudnesses per note.** Most were recorded played softly and loudly, some at three or four loudnesses (the clarinet, horn, trombone, pianos and basses), and the velocity picks the recording, so a soft note has the tone of a softly played instrument, not a loud one turned down. The recordings' levels are matched, so velocity still sets the loudness smoothly.
- **Several takes per note.** Short notes, plucked strings and guitars have two takes of each note that play in turn, so repeated notes, strummed rhythms and ostinatos don't sound copy-pasted.
- **In tune and even.** Every recording was measured and corrected to true pitch (the pianos keep their stretched upper octaves, as real pianos have), brass and wind notes that scoop up into pitch were bent onto it, and notes much louder or quieter than their neighbours were evened out.
- **In stereo.** The recordings keep both sides as they were recorded, so a string section is as wide as the section and a piano spreads from low to high, with the room they were played in (the basses were recorded straight from the instrument, in mono, and stay so; the guitars, recorded straight from the pickup or in a dead room, play in a small studio room, after the amp, as a microphone in front of the speaker hears one).
- **Held as long as you like.** A held note plays its recording, then repeats a seamless stretch of its steady part for as long as the note (or your key) lasts: a pad of strings can hold for a minute.
- **Let ring.** The pianos ring on for as long as a real one does with the pedal down (up to 24 seconds, until each note has died away), the harp up to 14, the tubular bells 16, the vibraphone 12 and the celesta 10. Where a library stopped its recording before the note had died away, it rings on at the rate it was dying away.
- **Smooth between loudnesses.** A velocity right at the boundary between two recordings plays both, blended, so notes either side of it don't jump from one tone to the other.
- **Letting go.** The **Harpsichord**, **Concert Grand** and **Upright Piano** make the sound a key makes as it's let go (the harpsichord's jack falling back, a piano's damper settling on the string) when a note ends before it has rung out, at the level it was recorded with the note (a piano's quieter the longer the note was held). The **Electric**, **Crunch**, **Distortion** and **Hollow-Body Guitar (Real)** do the same with the guitar's own recordings of a string being damped, so short, chopped chords sound played.
- **Guitars are strummed.** A chord written as one block (all its notes at the same moment) on a guitar, banjo or mandolin is strummed: the pick crosses the strings a few milliseconds apart, low to high, quicker the harder it's played, the last strings a touch softer. A chord following closely on another is the hand coming back up (high to low, its top strings loudest), so 8th- and 16th-note strumming goes down and up as a player's hand does; after a pause the hand starts down again. Chords you've strummed yourself (the piano roll's **Strum** tools, or notes placed apart) play as written.
- **Players breathe.** Wind and brass notes held longer than a player's breath (about 6 to 9 seconds) fade gently instead of holding forever (switch **Breathe** off in the track's **Ring** panel for a drone that never tires).
- **Ring** (the Track Inspector, on any recorded instrument): notes ring on after they end, dying away over the time you set (0.3 to 30 seconds), for long, substantial notes in dramatic and ambient music without drawing a sustain pedal lane. An instrument that rings by itself (a piano, the harp, bells, plucked strings) rings on as if the pedal were down for that long, then is damped; a bowed, blown or sung note fades away as a string left to sound in a hall does. Off, notes stop as the instrument's own do when let go. It changes from the next note, so you can try it while the song plays.
- **Sections aren't robots.** In the string and brass sections and the choir, the notes of a chord start a few milliseconds apart, as no two players start together.

The instruments:

- **Strings**: **Violins**, **Violas** and **Cellos (Real)**, sections bowed with vibrato, each with a **Short** version playing short, bouncing strokes (spiccato) for ostinatos; **Pizzicato Section** and **Tremolo Section** (cellos, violas and violins across the range); a **Solo Violin**, a bowed **Double Bass** and a concert **Harp**. **Violin**, **Viola**, **Cello** and **Contrabass (Real)** are single players recorded on every note at three loudnesses (soft, medium and loud), so nothing is stretched between notes; they were recorded without vibrato in a dead room, so the app gives each held note a player's vibrato (coming in after the note starts, a little different every time, with the shimmer of the instrument's body) and a small stage around it. The bowed strings play as a string player would: a held note's bow swells in (slower the softer it's played), a short note starts past the bow's slow start, so fast runs speak instead of swelling in; a note written to overlap the one before is a **legato** change, sliding into the new note without a fresh bow attack (overlap the notes in the piano roll to get it); and a solo violin, viola or cello plays at most two notes of a chord at once (a double stop: the lowest and highest). The violins have a string player's EQ (the 2.5 and 4 kHz bite taken down), the single players a hall's softening of the bite a close microphone hears, and the reverb, as a real hall does, soaks up the very top and the boom. A new orchestral track starts with more of the hall's reverb than other tracks (strings on records sit in a hall); turn its **Reverb** send down for a drier sound.
- **Woodwinds**: **Flute** (and **Flute Short** for tongued notes), **Oboe**, **Clarinet** and **Bassoon**, and **Recorder (Real)**: wooden baroque recorders played straight, the tenor at the bottom, the alto in the middle and the soprano at the top.
- **Brass**: **French Horn**, **Trumpet** and **Trombone**, each with a **Short** version for stabs and fanfares, **Tuba**, and **Muted Trumpet (Real)**, the trumpet with a harmon mute, soft and loud. The **French Horn (Real)** is recorded on every note from A#1 to F5 at three loudnesses; its **Short** version stops at D#4 (that library has nothing between there and D5), so for short notes higher up use the **Brass Section**.
- **Sections**: **String Section (Real)** is the cellos, violas and violins as one instrument (cellos at the bottom, violins at the top), for pads, swells and film chords; **Brass Section (Real)** is the trombone, horn and trumpet the same way. Each section is levelled so it sounds as one group across the keyboard.
- **Percussion**: **Timpani (Real)**, five kettle drums at three loudnesses (notes from D#2 to A#3; a starter's lower or higher hits move by octaves onto the drums).
- **Keys and mallets**: a **Concert Grand** at four loudnesses, an **Upright Piano**, a Flemish **Harpsichord**, a church **Pipe Organ** with its pedal pipes and a gentler **Chapel Organ**, and **Marimba**, **Xylophone**, **Glockenspiel**, **Vibraphone** (soft mallets), **Tubular Bells**, a **Kalimba** and a **Celesta** recorded key by key.
- **Band**: **Alto Sax**, **Tenor Sax** and **Baritone Sax**, a plucked **Upright Bass** and a fingered **Electric Bass**, and the guitars: **Electric Guitar (Real)**, **Crunch Guitar (Real)** and **Distortion Guitar (Real)** (one recorded electric through three amps: clean and bright, pushed until it breaks up, and high gain), **Hollow-Body Guitar (Real)** through a warm clean amp, **Flatwound Guitar (Real)**, warm and round for jazz and soul, and **Classical Guitar (Real)**, a nylon-string guitar recorded on every note, soft, medium and hard, for Spanish, bossa nova and fingerpicking. **Acoustic Guitar (Real)** plays the same recordings voiced brighter for strumming.

The electric guitars were recorded straight from the pickup, as guitarists do when they want to choose the amp later, and each instrument adds its amp and speaker cabinet in the app, voiced as a miked amp sounds on a record (the speaker's narrow peak near 4 kHz, which a close microphone makes piercing, taken down). Chords go through the amp together, as they do on a real amp, so a distorted chord grinds the way it should. The three amp versions share one set of recordings, so using all three downloads it once. Their hardest-picked recordings, much brighter than the rest, play only for accents (velocities over about 0.88; a new note's 0.8 plays the medium ones), and a held note rings on for 5 or 6 seconds, dying away as a string does.

They come from the site the app is published on (about 290 MB in all, fetched a set at a time the first time you use each instrument, 1 to 10 MB each and up to 32 MB for a piano, then kept in this browser) and are free to use (see Credits). The older soundfont instruments are now **retro instruments**, hidden from the lists unless you show them (see *Retro instruments* below); songs that use them sound as they did. To hear a song with the real ones, change the track's instrument in the Track Inspector (they start out at about the same loudness as the instrument they replace). The starters play the recordings: their strings, brass, winds, harp, guitars, basses, electric piano, organ, mallets, celesta, timpani, sax, muted trumpet, recorder, banjo, harmonica, ocarina, steel drums and acoustic drum kit are the **(Real)** instruments (the short-stroke versions for parts of short notes), and their soft pads are Synth Designer pads (the **Glass pad**, **Halo pad**, **Sweep pad**, **Polysynth** and **Soundtrack pad** presets). A few sounds have no free recording yet and are still retro ones in the starters: taiko, music box and much of the World group. The **Studio Kit (Real)** is a full recorded drum kit (kick, snare, hats, three toms, crash and ride, each at four loudnesses with two takes). So are the **Session Kit (Real)** (a kit with more of its room, plus a rimshot on the Clap row and a cowbell) and the **Brush Kit (Real)** (the same pieces played with brushes, a rimshot on the Clap row and a woodblock on the Cowbell row). **Latin Percussion (Real)** puts hand percussion on a kit's rows, and the piano roll names them: tumba and conga on Kick and Snare, claves, shaker and tambourine, low and high bongo and a timbale on the toms, triangle, agogô bell and cowbell. **Percussion Extras (Real)** is the rest of that percussion, to layer on a second track: low timbale, low and high woodblock, slow and fast guiro, cabasa, sleigh bells, vibraslap, bell tree, whistle and low agogô. **Choir (Real)** is a choir singing "aah", soft and loud: twenty trained singers (the women from G3 up, the men up to F4, both together in between), each moved onto every note from the nearest one they sang, keeping their own voice, vowel and vibrato, and spread across the stage as a choir stands; **Choir "Ooh" (Real)** is the same singers on a soft, round "ooh", for pads, lullabies and film scores. **Solo Voice (Real)** is one low male voice singing "aah". There are more pianos besides the Concert Grand and Upright: the **Studio Grand (Real)**, close and clear, and the **Old Upright (Real)**, a little out of tune on purpose. The orchestra's low and high winds are recorded too: **Piccolo**, **Alto Flute**, **Bass Flute**, **Bass Clarinet**, **Bass Trombone** and a **Muted French Horn**, plus a **Soprano Sax (Real)** with vibrato. From around the world and the unusual: a **Folk Harp**, a **Glass Harp** (wine glasses sung with a wet finger), a **Mbira** and a **Balafon** from Africa, a **Bowed Psaltery**, **Hand Chimes**, a **Gong** at four loudnesses and a **Didgeridoo** drone (one note, held as long as you like, up to three semitones either way). The brightest of them (the **Glockenspiel**, **Harpsichord**, **Harmonica**, **Muted Trumpet** and **Trombone Short (Real)**, and the Studio Kit's hats, cymbals and snare) are kept at twice the detail, so notes played away from their recorded pitch stay clear and sparkling; they take twice the memory (except on computers with 4 GB or less). Turn **Expressive playing** on (it is on for new tracks): with real recordings it filters the soft notes much less, because the soft recording already sounds soft.

### Retro instruments

Fledgetone started out on the free General MIDI soundfonts of the 1990s (FluidR3 and Musyng Kite) and a few early sample sets. Those 128 instruments are still here, all in one group, **Retro (1990s soundfonts)**, but the instrument lists hide it so the recordings and synths come first. Each group in a list starts with its recorded instruments.

- **Show them**: at the bottom of any instrument list (Add Track, or the instrument at the top of the Track Inspector), click **Show … retro instruments (1990s soundfonts)**. The Retro group appears at the end of the list, and searching finds them too (a search that only matches retro ones offers the same switch). **Hide the retro instruments** puts them away again. The choice is remembered in this browser.
- **Songs keep playing them.** A song or starter that uses a retro instrument plays it as before, and a track on one always shows it in its own list. Pocket, the phone version, lists only the others.
- They include sounds with no recording here yet: General MIDI pads and synth choirs, synth leads and organs, most of the World instruments, and the sound effects (seashore, bird, telephone, helicopter, applause and more).

### Expressive playing

A recorded instrument has one recording per note, made at one loudness, so on its own a soft note is just the same note turned down, and every repeat is identical: that's much of what makes sampled music sound like an old game. **Expressive playing** (a tick box under the instrument in the Track Inspector) plays the recordings the way the instruments really behave:

- **Softer is darker.** Each note gets its own tone filter that follows its velocity: at full velocity the recording is untouched; soft notes lose their brightness as well as their level (by about two to three octaves of top end at the softest, depending on the instrument family, brass and strings the most, pads the least). So a crescendo drawn with velocities brightens as it grows, as a real one does.
- **A natural loudness curve.** Velocity follows a decibel curve rather than a straight line, so the soft end is really soft.
- **No machine-gun repeats.** Every note is a hair different, up to ±3 cents in pitch and ±0.6 dB in level, worked out from the note and its moment so exports come out the same every time. On the drum kits, each hit is detuned a little (the kick hardly at all, the hats and claps the most) and its level varies, as a real drummer's hits do.
- **A modern 808.** The 808 Bass becomes a clean mono sub with a separate driven copy for the grit, low end untouched by the distortion, with a softer attack (6 ms).

It's on for every track of a new song and every track you add. Songs made before it existed open with it off, so they sound as they did; tick it to try. Velocities matter more with it on: draw accents (0.85-0.95), normal notes (0.55-0.7) and ghosts (0.25-0.35), and use the **HUMAN** insert for timing.

For **classical and angelic** music: **Choir (Real)** and **Choir "Ooh" (Real)** for heavenly voices, a Synth Designer **Halo pad**, **Glass pad** or **Angelic pad** underneath, **Harp (Real)**, **Celesta (Real)** and **Glockenspiel (Real)** for sparkle, and the Orchestra group for everything from a string quartet (Violin, Viola, Cello, Contrabass) to a full orchestra with woodwinds, brass and Timpani.

- **Recorded instruments** download their sounds the first time you use them, so they need an internet connection then; after that they're kept in this browser and load at once, even offline (Master tab › **Instruments offline** can download them all ahead of time). A spinner shows on the track header while they load, and the Inspector shows **Loading CDN...**, **Ready** or **Error**. When a song opens, each instrument fetches the recordings of the notes the song plays first, and plays as soon as those have arrived; the rest download behind it, one at a time, and join in as they come (an instrument added to a song starts with one loudness of every note). A note whose own recording hasn't arrived yet plays the nearest one that has, while its own is fetched. Exports wait for every recording the song plays. To save memory, recordings are kept without their silent ends, and ones whose two sides are the same sound are kept as one; neither changes how anything sounds. On a phone or a computer with little memory (4 GB or less), the rest of an instrument's recordings download only when a note needs them. If a download fails, the track header shows a red **!** in place of its coloured dot and the Inspector shows **Error** with a **Retry** button; click either to try again. Failed downloads are also retried when your connection comes back, and when you hear it with ▶ in the instrument menu, add another track with it or export. Once it downloads, every track using it plays from its next note, with no reload.
- **Tuning**: every recording of the recorded instruments was measured for pitch, and the ones that were out of tune (some soundfont recordings are off by up to half a semitone, so neighbouring notes clashed) are re-pitched as they load, or replaced by a steadier recording a semitone away. Across the instruments, 99.8% of notes are within 10 cents (a tenth of a semitone) of true pitch. **Pizzicato Strings** now uses a steadier set of recordings, one for every key. Bells, drums and the piano (tuned slightly wide on purpose, as real pianos are) are left as recorded.
- **Balance across the range**: every instrument's level was measured in its low, middle and top register with a weighting close to how ears hear at normal volume. Instruments that got louder or harsher up high play their top notes softer (a taper), the brightest ones (Flute, Recorder, Pan Flute, Ocarina, Violin, Erhu, Saxophone) start a little quieter, and the deep instruments and strings (Cello, Viola, Contrabass, Strings, Tremolo, French Horn, Trombone, Tuba, Bassoon, the basses, the choirs) have their slow recorded swell trimmed so notes speak at once, more upper-mid presence and less low-mid boom, so they sound close instead of in the next room.
- Every drum kit has the same eleven rows: **Kick**, **Snare**, **Clap**, **Closed Hat**, **Open Hat**, **Tom Low**, **Tom Mid**, **Tom High**, **Cowbell**, **Ride** and **Crash**, so drum parts move between kits. The recorded kits play their own recordings of the kick, snare, hats and toms, and synthesized hits for the rows they have no recording of. On a MIDI keyboard, the drum pads land on the matching rows. Taiko and Timpani are played like pitched instruments.
- Slap Bass, Sub-Bass, 808 Bass and Analog Lead play one note at a time; in a chord, only the loudest note sounds. On the Sub-Bass and Analog Lead a note drawn to start where the last one ends (or overlapping it) carries on from it without a new attack: it glides to its pitch, or holds on as one note when it's the same pitch (a tie). To hit a note again, leave a gap before it. The **808 slides**: a note that starts before the previous one has ended glides to its pitch instead of hitting again (the sliding 808 of rap beats), so draw the first note long enough to overlap the next. Notes that only touch hit separately. To shape the 808 (drive, punch, slide time, tail), see **808 designer** below. Space Clav (16 notes at once), Wavetable Synth (12) and Brass Stabs (8) cut short whichever ringing note is quietest when they run out (a note already let go before one still held, never one that has only just started unless every note has), so new notes still play (only notes beyond the limit that start at the very same moment are dropped). A recorded instrument rings up to 64 notes at once; past that, the one quietest at that moment fades out quickly, worked out from how hard it was played, how far it has died away and whether its key is still down.

### Synth Designer

**Synth Designer** (Keys & Synths) is a synth you build yourself. Its controls appear in the Track Inspector under the instrument:

- **Load preset…** starts from a sound: Init saw, Supersaw lead, Wobble bass, Growl bass, Sub bass, Angelic pad, Warm pad, Glass pad, Halo pad, Sweep pad, Polysynth, Soundtrack pad, Pluck, Bell, Brass, 8-bit lead, Riser noise, **Analog poly**, **FM bell**, **FM electric piano**, **Organ**, **Vowel lead**, **Pulse bass**, **Rave hoover** (three saws slightly out of tune with a saw an octave under, gliding between notes; add the **Scoop** tool for the classic swoop), **Reese bass** (detuned saws beating slowly over a sine sub, a warm filter drifting open and closed over each bar), **Dark reese (heavy)** (wider detune, harder drive, the filter moving every half bar) or **Formant growl** (a vowel wave bent by FM through a resonant band-pass that talks in time). The bass presets keep their low end centred.
- **Osc 1** and **Osc 2**: a wave each (Saw, Square, Triangle, Sine, **Pulse** (narrow, nasal and hollow), **Organ** (drawbar harmonics) or **Vowel** (an "ah", vocal)), tuned by octave, semitone and cents, with a level. **Sub** adds a sine an octave down, **Noise** adds white noise.
- **Unison** stacks up to 7 copies of each oscillator, detuned across **Spread** and panned across **Width**, for big wide leads and pads. Unison saws start at different points of their cycle, as analog oscillators do, so a stack doesn't sweep ("zip") as it opens up.
- **Filter**: Low-pass, High-pass, Band-pass or **Ladder** (a warm 24 dB low-pass, driven a little and resonant, as on classic analog synths), with **Cutoff**, **Reso**(nance), how far its own envelope opens it (**Env amt**, in octaves), how much it follows the note (**Key trk**) and **Drive** for grit (shaped at four times the sample rate, so it doesn't fizz). **Drift** makes each note's oscillators a few cents off from each other and its filter a little brighter or darker, as no two notes on an analog synth are quite alike. **FM** lets Osc 2 bend Osc 1's pitch at audio rate, for bells, electric pianos and metallic basses (Osc 2's own level can be at 0: it still bends Osc 1).
- **Filter envelope** and **Amp envelope**: Attack, Decay, Sustain, Release.
- **LFO**: wobbles the **Filter**, adds vibrato to the **Pitch** or tremolo to the **Volume**, in sync with the tempo (1/1 to 1/32, triplets too) or free in Hz. Each note restarts it, so a synced filter LFO is a dubstep wobble locked to the beat.
- **Mono** plays one note at a time and **Glide**s between them; **Level** sets the output.

Every change is one Undo step (a whole slider drag is one), is saved with the project and plays from the next note. Double-click a slider to reset it.

### 808 designer

Select an **808 Bass** track: the Track Inspector shows the **808 designer**. Off, the track plays the original 808. Switch it **ON** to shape it (one undo step; switch it off to go back):

- **Load preset…**: **Clean deep** (a pure, long sine sub), **Punchy** (a hard pitch drop and a short tail that hits like a kick), **Distorted** (warm saturation that growls on small speakers), **Blown-out** (clipped flat and loud), **Long slide** (a long tail with slow, wide slides) or **Dark (room for cowbell)** (folded grit kept dark, under the cowbell, with a tight tail).
- **Drive** takes it from a clean sine (0%) to clipped and distorted (100%); **Character** is how: **Warm tube**, **Hard clip**, **Fold** (the wave folds back on itself, a buzzier growl) or **Bitcrush**. Turning the drive up adds grit rather than level. **Tone** is a low-pass after the distortion: lower is darker. The distortion is worked out at four times the sample rate, so it stays clean instead of fizzing.
- **Top layer** adds the distortion's harmonics above the sub (200 Hz to 4 kHz), so the 808 is heard on phones and laptop speakers that can't play the sub itself.
- **Punch** starts each hit up to 36 semitones above the note and drops it to the note over **Punch time**: the knock at the front of an 808.
- **Glide**: how long a slide takes. A note that starts before the previous one ends slides to its pitch instead of hitting again.
- **Decay**: how long a held note takes to die away (0.1 to 8 s). A note also stops where it ends.
- **Level**: the 808's output.
- **Tune to key** snaps every note to the nearest note of a key while it plays, so a stray note can't clash with the song (your notes aren't changed). It starts on the song's key, worked out from its other parts; change the key and scale beside it.

The 808 is mono (the same in both speakers), and its whole sound starts a few milliseconds early to make up for the distortion's delay, so it lands on the beat. Every setting is saved with the project and plays from the next note. (With the designer on, **Expressive playing** doesn't change the 808: the designer's settings are its sound.)

**Kick and bass**: on bass tracks, a panel says how many of the song's kicks land on a low note of the track (a low bass hitting with the kick hides the kick's punch). **Duck under the kick** fixes it in one click (one undo step): it switches on the track's **Pump**, following the kick, deep and quick (80%, 150 ms), so the bass dips at each kick and swells straight back. On the demo beat, that took the 808 down 10 dB in the first 60 ms of each kick and less than 1 dB overall.

### Heavy bass tools

- **Glide** (Slap Bass, Sub-Bass, Analog Lead, Wobble, Growl and Reese Bass): switch it on in the Track Inspector and notes that overlap (as the **Legato** tool draws them: each runs a little into the next) slide into each other over **Time** (5 ms to 1 s) without a new attack; notes that only touch or have a gap start fresh, so a bassline of repeated notes keeps its rhythm. On the Slap Bass it also stops the pluck on joined notes. Off, each instrument keeps its own slide (the Lead's short glide, the EDM basses' slides). The Synth Designer has its own **Mono** and **Glide**.
- **Sub enhancer** (bass instruments, the Synth Designer, the Sampler, Multi Sampler and plugins): a clean sine under every note, centred, for a thin bass. **Where**: **An octave below** (a new, deeper floor; below about 28 Hz it plays on the note instead, since lower is felt, not heard) or **On the note** (reinforces a weak fundamental). **Level**, and **Decay** (Hold for the whole note, or a time to die away, for plucky basses). It follows the notes, so it's always in tune and in time, live and exported. In a chord it plays under the lowest note.
- **Bass Drive** (an insert, after Distortion): the heavy bass trick of distorting only the mids and highs. Below the **Crossover** (60 to 300 Hz, 120 to start) the sound stays clean and is made mono; above it, **Drive** with a **Character** (Warm tube, Hard clip, Fold, Bitcrush), a **Tone** low-pass on the distortion and a **Mix** with the clean highs. The two parts add back up exactly (at 0% mix the track sounds as it did). On a reese it leaves the sub where it was and brings the mids up about 6 dB: much louder on a phone, a little louder overall.

### Drum Designer: your own hard drums

**Designed Kit** (Drums & Percussion, or **Tools > Drum Designer**) is a drum kit you make yourself, synthesized in the browser: every drum row (kick, snare, clap, closed and open hat, three toms, perc, crash and ride) plays the sound you design for it. Its controls appear in the Track Inspector:

- **Pick a row** (clicking it plays it), then load a **Preset** or shape it. Kicks: **Punch kick**, **Hardcore kick**, **Deep tail kick**, **Club kick**, **Crunch kick** (a hard-driven body whose pitch drop you can hear and a loud click, its tail short so a kick a 16th later stays clear: for dense, syncopated drops), **Zap kick** (a longer, rubbery drop), **Hard drop kick** (for future-bass drops: a loud click and a driven knock you hear on small speakers too, falling onto a tuned sub that carries the low end for about a quarter of a second before the bass takes over), **Drop impact** (one deep boom with a burst of noise over it that rings on for a second or two: put it on the first downbeat of a drop) (layered, see below), **Hard hitting kick**, **Punchy short kick**, **Distorted boom kick**, **Tight kick**, **Clicky sub kick**, **Dusty lo-fi kick**. Snares: **Big snare**, **Gated snare**, **Tight snare**, **Crunch snare** (tight and driven hard) (layered), **Punchy snare**, **Crisp snare**, **Fat layered snare**, **Tight dry snare**, **Dusty lo-fi snare**. Claps: **Crack clap** (layered), **Crisp clap**, **Big room clap**, **Snappy short clap**, **Clap and snare stack**. Hats: **Tight closed hat**, **Sharp tight hat**, **Bright hat**, **Dark closed hat**, **Open hat**, **Short open hat** (open and bright but gone before the next beat: the off-beat hat of house), **Long sizzling open hat**. Cymbals: **Washy crash**, **Ping ride**. Percussion: **Wood rim**, **Sharp rim**, **Low**, **Mid** and **High tom**, **Shaker**, **Double shake**, **Cowbell**.
- **A new Designed Kit** starts on the **Punch kick** and the **Big snare**: a short, clicky kick whose low end is gone in about a seventh of a second, so it leaves the sub to the bass, and a big snare that hits a little over it as you hear them. Its other rows, and the rows of a kit from before, play a hard modern kit until you design them (the bold rows are your own). **Reset row** puts a row back to where a new kit starts.
- **Each layer** is one voice with its own knobs. **Kick**: Punch (the click), Pitch, Sweep (how far above its pitch the body starts, in semitones) and Sweep time, Decay, Noise, Distortion. **Snare**: Tone, Body, Body decay, Noise, Noise color, Decay, Snap, Sweep, Distortion. **Clap**: Bursts (1 to 6 slaps), Spread (the time between them), Color, Tail, Snap. **Hat / cymbal**: Metal (six detuned square waves, as on analog drum machines), Color, Decay (short for a closed hat, long for an open one or a crash), Noise, Click. **Rim**, **Tom**, **Shaker** (with an attack and up to three shakes) and **Bell / cowbell** have theirs.
- **Layered kick**: three parts in one voice. The **Click** (a few milliseconds of noise; **Click tone** 1 to 5 kHz, **Click length** 3 to 15 ms), the **Body** (a sine, or a saw as you turn up **Body wave**, falling from its **Punch pitch** onto the tail pitch over the **Pitch drop** time; **Body decay**), and the **Sub**, a clean sine on the tail pitch that rings for **Tail** (the time it takes to fall 20 dB). **Body drive** distorts the body only (**Drive type**: Soft, Hard or Fold), through a **Cabinet** low-pass, so the sub under it stays clean. **Hardcore tail** keeps the driven body going through the beat at the Tail's length (at 163 BPM: a few dB down through the beat, about 20 dB down by 290 ms), so the kick is the bass. **Tail note**: **Song key** puts the tail on the song's key root (the octave nearest 45 to 66 Hz) and keeps it there when the notes change; or pick a note.
- **Layered snare**: a **Transient** (a crack that falls 12 dB in about 10 ms), a tuned **Body** (**Body note**: the song's key, near 120 to 170 Hz, or a note), the **Noise** of the wires (**Noise color** where it sits, **Noise tone** how bright, **Noise decay**), a **Clap** layer and a **Room** that rings for **Room length**. **Gated room** is the 80s snare: the room holds for an 8th at the song's tempo, then stops dead (with tempo changes, at the tempo most of that kit's snares play at).
- Every drum sits under a fixed peak, and the drives are levelled, so no setting can make a hit jump out.
- **Add layer** stacks a second voice on the drum (a clap on a snare, a rim click on a sub kick), with its own **Level** and **Delay** (up to 40 ms after the first).
- **Level** moves the whole drum up or down from where it sits. Every drum is made to its row's loudness, near the other kits' (a kick about as loud as the Drum Machine's, the snare and clap a couple of dB under the kick as you hear them, hats as the recorded kits'), and turning up Distortion makes it denser and harder, not louder, so switching presets never jumps out of the mix.
- The closed and open hats are one hi-hat: a hat hit cuts an open hat still ringing.

**Synthesized kits**: **Techno Kit** (a driven kick, a bright snare and clap, tight hats), **Retro Drum Machine** (an 80s-style machine: a round kick, a snare with some room), **Rhythm Box** (a vintage rhythm box: a soft thump, ticking hats, bongo-like toms) and **Break Kit** (crunchy break-style hits) are Drum Designer kits ready to play, on the usual rows (C1 kick, D1 snare, and so on). **Edit in Drum Designer** in the Track Inspector turns the track into a Designed Kit starting from that kit's sounds. Switching a Designed Kit of your own to another instrument and back to Designed Kit gives you your own design back. They took the place of the recorded Acoustic Kit, Breakbeat Kit, 80s Drum Machine, Rhythm Box, Techno Kit and Bongos, whose recordings came with no licence: a song or link that used one opens and plays on its replacement (the 80s Drum Machine on the Retro Drum Machine, the old Rhythm Box, Techno and Breakbeat kits on the new ones, the Acoustic Kit on the **Studio Kit (Real)** and the Bongos on **Latin Percussion (Real)**), with its notes on the same rows.

Each change plays the drum so you hear it, is one Undo step (a whole slider drag is one) and is saved with the project. The sounds are rendered once and replayed, so the kit costs next to nothing to play, sounds exactly the same live and in an export, and an export repeats sample for sample. **Expressive playing** makes every hit a hair different in pitch and level, the same way on every play.

### EDM instruments

- **Wobble Bass**: the dubstep wobble, a filter sweeping in time with the beat. **Growl Bass**: the talking riddim growl, a vowel filter ("oo", "ah", "eh") swept the same way. **Reese Bass**: detuned saws that beat slowly, for drum & bass and dubstep.
- These three play one note at a time (the loudest wins). A note that starts before the previous one ends, or right where it ends, **slides** to its pitch without re-attacking (the same pitch carries on as one note, the wobble starting again with it). Leave a gap to hit a note again.
- **WOBBLE** in the piano roll header (Wobble and Growl only) sets the wobble rate for the whole clip: **1/1**, **1/2**, **1/4**, **1/8** (default), **1/8T**, **1/16**, **1/16T** or **1/32**. Each note restarts the sweep, so wobbles stay locked to the beat. Different clips can use different rates; the rate shows as a small tag on the clip, is saved with the project, and travels with copies. Changing it is one undo step.
- **Supersaw**: seven detuned saws per note, spread wide (with everything under about 180 Hz kept in the middle), for house and trance chords, with a release that carries one chord into the next (about 1.5 s). **Pluck**: a short, bright pluck for melodies and arps. Both play chords. The **Wavetable Synth** is widened to stereo too and fades over about 1.5 s; the **Analog Lead** is gently widened, with a short tail between phrases. (The retro synth pads, synth strings and synth leads are widened the same way.)
- **FX** has fixed rows like a drum kit: **Riser**, **Noise Sweep**, **Reverse Crash**, **Impact**, **Sub Drop** and **Downlifter**. Every effect except Impact lasts as long as the note you draw: draw a 4-bar note for a 4-bar riser, and end a Reverse Crash on the drop. Impact is a one-shot boom of about 3.5 s. In an FX clip, NEW NOTE starts at 1 bar, and clicking a row's name previews it. FX clips only go on FX tracks.
- **Drop Pad** (in FX) plays no sound of its own: each of its rows does something to the **whole mix** for as long as a note on it lasts. **Stutter** repeats the 16th that was playing; **Filter** sweeps the mix down to a muffle over a bar (and opens again over two 16ths after); **Tape stop** slows it to a halt over a beat; **Crush** makes it gritty and lo-fi; **Gate** chops it into 16ths; **Echo** throws what's playing into an echo that rings on after the note ends. Draw notes on its rows like any part, record them by holding the pads in Pocket, or play them from a MIDI or computer keyboard on an armed Drop Pad track (C4 is Stutter and each key up is the next effect, round again after F4). They play the same in exports, after the master's OTT and width and before its limiter. Drop Pad notes play exactly on the beat (swing and Human don't move them), a key change leaves them where they are, and muting the track stops its moves from the next one on (one already going finishes). One Drop Pad track is enough for a song. A track with clips can't be switched to the Drop Pad or from it (its notes would change meaning): add a new track instead.
- Right-click an empty spot on a lane of these instruments for ready-made parts: **Wobble Riff**, **Growl Riff**, **Reese Line**, **Trance Chords**, **Pluck Arp**, and for FX a **Build-Up** and a **Drop Hit**.

### Chiptune

The sound of 8-bit game consoles, for retro games:

- **Chip Lead**: a 25% pulse wave, the classic 8-bit lead. Notes start and stop hard, and a vibrato comes in after a note has sounded a moment. It plays chords, so it does arpeggios too: fast 16th notes cycling through a chord are the chip way of playing one.
- **Chip Bass**: the stepped triangle wave those consoles played bass on. Like the real thing it has no fade: notes are on or off.
- **Chip Drums**: a noise kit on the same eleven rows as the other kits (its clap, toms and cymbals are the shared synthesized hits): a pitch-swept kick, a noise snare, short noise hats, and a metallic open hat and cowbell from the noise channel's "short mode".

For the authentic mono sound, switch the master's **Stereo Width** on and drag **Width** to 0 (**Mono**).

### The Sampler: your own sound

1. Add a track with **Your Sounds > Sampler** (or switch a track to Sampler).
2. In the Inspector, click **Load a sound…** and pick an audio file (WAV, MP3, OGG and most others) up to 50 MB.
3. Under **ORIGINAL PITCH ON**, choose which C (C2, C3, C4, C5 or C6; C4 by default) plays the file at its normal pitch. Higher notes play it faster and higher, lower notes slower and lower.
4. Write notes for it in the piano roll like any other instrument.

The sound is kept in this browser and saved inside the project file (see **Where your sounds live** below). It plays only as long as each note lasts (then fades out quickly), so draw long notes for long sounds. The play button previews a half note at the original pitch; the trash button removes the sound (`Ctrl+Z` brings it back).

### Drum Rack: your own drum sounds

**Add Track > Your Sounds > Drum Rack** gives 16 pads, each playing a sound of your own, laid out like a pad controller (pad 1, C2, bottom left; pad 16, D#3, top right).

- **Kit folder…** (or drop a folder on the pads) loads a kit from a folder of drum sounds, each on its pad by what it is: kick on pad 1, snare 2, clap 3, rim 4, closed and open hat 5 and 6, toms 7 to 9, crash 10, ride 11, perc 12, shaker 13, 808 14, snap 15, FX 16. What a sound is comes from its name ("Kick 01", "BD_3", "HH Closed", "OpenHat") or the folder it's in ("Kicks", "Hats"); pads the folder has nothing for get spare kicks, snares and hats.
- **Load sounds**: **Load sounds…** in its Inspector panel fills the pads in order with the files you pick (up to 16). Drop files on a pad to load them from there on, or select a pad and click **Load…**. Each pad is named after its file (rename it in the name box above its settings).
- **Each pad's own effects**: select a pad (clicking it plays it) for its **Level**, **Pan**, **Pitch** (±24 semitones), **Decay** (how long it rings before fading; "full" plays the whole sound), **Low-pass**, **High-pass**, **Drive** (saturation) and **reverse**.
- **Choke groups**: pads in the same group cut each other off, as a closed hi-hat stops an open one ringing. Put the open and closed hats in group 1.
- **Chops**: **Chop sample…** on an audio clip fills a new rack with chops of one sample (see Flipping a sample); a chop's pad also has **Start**, **End** and **Fade**.
- **Bounce to a pad** (resampling): turn part of your song into a new sound to play. Set the loop's **In** and **Out** around it, select a pad and click **From loop**: everything playing there becomes one sound on that pad, as loud and at the same pitch as the song plays it (a very loud mix plays a little quieter, never louder). Then pitch it, reverse it, shorten it with Decay, or play it in a new rhythm. **Track › Bounce bars … (In → Out) to a Drum Rack pad** does the same onto the first empty pad of the selected Drum Rack (or of any, or a new **Bounces** track), and a track's right-click menu has **Bounce In → Out to a pad** for that track alone (as it sounds in the mix, with its level, pan and sends). The bounce is exactly the bars, so it loops in time with them; it leaves out the master section, which the pad plays through again. One Undo takes it all off (the sound, the pad and any new track). A pad at its neutral settings plays its sound exactly as it is: its filters and drive only come in once you turn them.
- In the piano roll each pad is a row, named as you named it, with the **STEPS** step sequencer. A MIDI keyboard plays the pads on C2 to D#3, and any other octave lands on them too.
- The rack's sounds are kept like the Sampler's (see Where your sounds live) and saved inside the project file. The whole track also has the usual mixer, inserts and sends.

### Loading a sample pack

**Add Track > Sample pack (folder)…** (or drop the pack's folder in the space under the tracks, or search "sample pack") sorts a whole downloaded pack into tracks:

- **Drums** (kicks, snares, hats… by their names or folders) become **Drum Rack** kits, laid out as above: the first kick, snare, hat… of the pack in the first kit, the next ones in a second, up to four kits.
- **An instrument's notes** (a folder of at least three files named by their notes, "Keys C3.wav", "Keys C4.wav"…) become a **Multi Sampler**.
- **Loops** (named "loop", "break", or with a tempo like "140 BPM") go end to end on an **Audio track**, muted so they don't play over your song: unmute it to audition them and drag the ones you want onto your tracks.
- **Other one-shots** (a vocal "hey", a synth stab) go on the pads of a Drum Rack of their own (up to 32).

Up to 400 sounds are loaded from one pack; a message says what was made, and what was left out and why (files that aren't audio, files over 50 MB, sounds past the first 400). Each sound file can be up to 50 MB because the sounds are kept in this browser and saved inside the project file; drum hits and samples are far smaller, and a long recording saved as MP3 is a tenth of the size of a WAV.

### Multi Sampler: an instrument from your own recordings

**Add Track > Your Sounds > Multi Sampler** plays several of your sounds across the keyboard: record or collect a few notes of an instrument (a C every octave, or every few notes) and it becomes a playable instrument, each key using the nearest recording, played higher or lower.

- **Add sounds…** (or drop files on the panel, or **Folder…** / drop a folder for every sound in it). Each sound's own pitch is read from its file name ("Piano C4.wav", "Strings A#2.flac") or, if the name doesn't say, worked out by listening to it. Each then plays the keys nearest it.
- **Velocity layers**: sounds of the same note marked by loudness in their names (pp, p, mp, mf, f, ff, soft, hard) become layers: soft playing uses the soft recording, hard playing the hard one.
- The table lists each sound's **Root** (the key it plays at its own pitch), **Keys** (the lowest and highest it plays) and **Vel** (the softest and hardest velocity), all editable. Click a sound's name to hear it. **Re-map by root** lays them out again from their roots. **Attack** and **Release** shape every note.
- Pitch bend and per-note expression (see the piano roll) play on each note's own voice.

### Audio tracks: your own recordings

An **Audio** track holds sound files as clips instead of notes. Add one with **Add Track > Your Sounds > Audio**.

- **Getting a file in** (WAV, MP3, OGG, FLAC, M4A and anything else your browser can play, up to 50 MB each): drag files from your computer onto an Audio track's lane (it snaps; hold `Shift` for no snap), or onto the strip under the tracks to make a new Audio track. You can also click **Load audio…** in the Inspector (clips go at the playhead), or double-click an Audio lane or right-click it > **Load audio file…** (clips go at that bar). Several files land end to end.
- A clip is as long as its sound, is named after the file, and plays it as recorded. It keeps its start bar and its length in seconds when you change the tempo, so its length in bars follows the tempo, unless it **follows the tempo** (below).
- **Follow tempo** (in the clip editor): tick it for a loop or a recording made at a set tempo, and the clip is **time-stretched** with the song's tempo: it keeps its pitch and its length in bars, playing faster or slower. Set **recorded at** to the tempo it was made at; the buttons beside it suggest tempos at which the clip lasts a whole number of bars. **×1.20** and so on shows how fast it's playing; past about 30% faster or slower (amber) hits start to smear. Drum hits are kept sharp and on the beat. While a new tempo's stretch is being made (a moment, after you stop dragging the tempo), the clip plays sped up like tape, in time but a little off pitch.
- **Crossfades**: overlap two audio clips on a track (drag one over the end of another) and they **crossfade** across the overlap: the earlier fades out as the later fades in, with equal-power curves so the level holds through it. The overlap is drawn as a yellow X. A clip lying wholly inside another plays on top of it instead (a layer). A crossfade longer than a clip's own fade replaces that fade.
- **Tune vocal** (right-click an audio clip, the clip editor, or **Tools › Tune vocal**): pulls a recorded voice (or any instrument playing one note at a time) onto the notes of a key, keeping its timing. Pick the **key** (it starts on the key the song's notes are in) and the scale: **major**, **minor**, **harmonic minor**, **phrygian**, the **pentatonics** or **chromatic** (any note); **Strength** is how far onto the note (100% lands right on it), **Speed** how fast it gets there, from **natural** (slides and vibrato kept) to **instant** (the hard tuned sound). **Natural** and **Hard tune** set both at once. The graph shows the pitch sung (grey) and tuned (colour) against the key's notes. **Hear tuned** and **Hear original** compare them; **Apply** makes a tuned copy for the clip (one Undo step). The original is kept: **Untune** in the same dialog goes back to it.
- **Editing** works as for note clips: move, trim, razor, copy and paste, duplicate, Repeat, Fill to loop Out, Insert/Delete bars, undo. Trimming the left edge makes the clip play from further into the sound. Audio clips only move between Audio tracks.
- **Fades**: drag the small square at either top corner of a clip (shown when the clip is wide enough).
- **Gain and fades**: double-click a clip (or right-click > **Gain and fades**) for the clip editor under the timeline: the waveform, **GAIN** (-24 to +24 dB), **Fade in**, **Fade out**, the sample's tempo and key, and **Chop sample…** (see Flipping a sample). `Esc` closes it.
- **Warp markers** (the clip editor's **Warp** tool): pin a moment of the sound (a drum hit, a word) and move it in time; the audio either side stretches to fit, at the same pitch. Double-click the waveform to add a marker, drag one sideways to move that moment (it snaps to 16ths; Shift for anywhere), right-click to remove it. Markers work while the clip follows the tempo: adding one to a clip that doesn't sets **Follow tempo** at the song's tempo, so nothing else changes. **Clear warp** removes them all.
- **Quantize audio**: finds the hits in the clip and pulls each onto the chosen grid (1/4 to 1/32) with warp markers, at 25 to 100% strength. Tighten a loose drum take or a strummed part; drag any marker to adjust, or Undo.
- **Gain envelope** (the **Gain envelope** tool): draw the clip's level over its sound, on top of its gain and fades: click to add a point, drag points up and down (-60 dB, silent, to +12 dB), right-click to remove one. Duck a breath, lift a quiet word. **Clear envelope** removes it.
- **Reverse**: plays the clip backwards (a reversed copy of its sound, the same stretch of it mirrored). Click **Reversed** to go back.
- **Normalize**: sets the clip's gain so its loudest moment peaks just under full scale.
- The track's mixer, effects, sends and automation all work on Audio tracks. Swing never moves audio clips. Pressing Play in the middle of a clip plays it from the right place, and exports are sample-accurate.

### Rap vocals

Tools for recording and mixing rap and sung vocals over a beat. They work on **Audio** tracks.

- **Vocal chain** (in the Inspector of an Audio track): one click puts a ready vocal chain on the track's inserts, with settings, as one Undo step. It replaces only that track's inserts (its fader, sends, plugins and clips stay); you can then change any insert as usual. The lit button shows which chain is on until you change one.
  - **Clean lead**: clear and upfront. Low cut, a little presence and air, steady compression, a de-esser, a short room.
  - **Aggressive lead**: in your face. Hard compression, bright, a little saturation (Bass Drive on the mids and highs), a tight slap echo, almost no room.
  - **Ad-lib**: thinner and off to the side (it pans the track a little right), with a bouncing delay. For the shouts and answers around the lead.
  - **Phone / radio**: squeezed into the middle band, gritty and crushed. For an intro, a skit or a hook that cuts through.
  - **Dark and wide**: warm, top rolled off, chorus width, long ping-pong echoes and a big dark room.
  - **Backing stack**: doubles and harmonies tucked behind the lead: thinner, glued, chorused, in the room.

  The chains are levelled against each other: on a test voice they all come out within about 2 LU of each other (Aggressive the loudest), and the S band (5 to 10 kHz) no more than half a dB stronger, against the rest of the voice, than it was dry: the brightening is kept off the S's.
- **Ad-lib track** (under the chains): a new Audio track called Ad-libs under this one, with the Ad-lib chain on, selected so the microphone records onto it. One Undo step.
- **Hard tune** (right-click an audio clip): the fast, robotic pitch correction. It opens Tune vocal at full strength and instant speed, on the key the song's notes are in; pick another key or scale, or slow the Speed towards natural. It holds a note while your voice hovers between two, so it steps cleanly rather than warbling. Like Tune vocal, **Apply** makes a tuned copy of the clip's sound: it's a processing step, not a live effect, so what you hear is exactly what exports play. Tune a take folder after flattening it.
- **Double this take** (right-click an audio clip): makes a new track under the take's with a double of it: two new performances, one left and one right, each phrase a little early or late (5 to 22 ms) and a few cents sharp or flat (3 to 9, the two sides opposite ways), playing 7 dB lower through the same chain. It sounds like the line was recorded again, not copied. The same take always doubles the same way, and the double plays the same in every export. One Undo step takes it away. Flatten a take folder first.
- **Loop-record takes**: tick **Loop passes are takes** in the recording settings (the arrow next to Record), set the loop round the bars and record from the microphone: every pass round the loop becomes a take of one clip, ready to comp (see Takes and comping).

### The Slicer: vocal chops

The **Slicer** (**Add Track > Your Sounds > Slicer**) cuts a sound into slices and puts one on each key from C3 up (Slice 1 on C3, Slice 2 on C#3, and so on, up to 64).

- In the Inspector, click **Load a sound…**. **CUT**: **Auto** cuts where it hears a new word or hit (**SENS** sets how faint a hit still starts a slice); **Equal** cuts the sound into 4, 8, 16 or 32 equal slices.
- On the waveform, drag a marker to move a cut, double-click to add one, and right-click a marker to remove it. Click a slice to hear it.
- In the piano roll the rows read **Slice 1**, **Slice 2** and so on. A note plays its slice from the start, for the note's length or the slice's length, whichever is shorter.
- **Pitch** mode: every key plays the slice you last clicked, pitched from its own key, so you can play a chop as a melody.
- **Chop sample…** (right-click an audio clip, or in the clip editor) has **To a Slicer instead**: a Slicer track from that clip's sound, cut where the dialog's markers are, with a clip that plays the slices in order, ready to rearrange. The audio clip stays.
- Slices play at the sound's own level; use the track fader to balance it.

### Flipping a sample

Tools for making a beat from a sample: a loop or a recording of your own (or one you have the rights to), dropped on an Audio track.

- **Its tempo and key**: the clip editor (double-click the clip) shows the tempo and key the app hears in the sample, each with how sure it is (amber when it's a guess; a key it isn't sure of shows the next likeliest too, often the relative major or minor). Once heard, the clip shows them too (bottom left: *88 · D min*), and the Audio track's panel lists each sound on the track with them. A sample's answer is worked out once and remembered in this browser.
- **Song to 88** sets the song's tempo to the sample's; the button beside it is the same beat counted the other way (half or double). **Stretch to song** makes the clip follow the song's tempo (time-stretched, same pitch; see Follow tempo above). **Into E min (+2 st)** moves the sample into the key the song's notes are in, by the nearest way (a minor sample in a major song goes to its relative minor), allowing for a sample that sits a few cents off: it makes a re-pitched copy, the same length and timing, as one Undo step. **Original key** goes back.
- **Chop sample…** (right-click the clip, or in the clip editor) cuts the part of the sound the clip plays into up to 16 chops:
  - **At the hits**: a chop starts at each drum hit, chord stab or word (**SENS**: how faint a hit still counts; the strongest 16 are kept). **Equal**: 4, 8, 12 or 16 equal parts (beats or bars of a loop). **By hand**: double-click the waveform to add a marker, drag one to move it, right-click to remove it. Dragging or adding a marker in the other modes takes them over by hand.
  - Click a chop to hear it. **Fade** (0 to 50 ms) smooths each chop's edges. **One at a time** (on): a chop cuts off the one playing, as on a classic sampler; off, they ring over each other.
  - **Chop to 16 pads** puts them on a new Drum Rack track under the sample's (chop 1 on pad 1, C2), so the pads, the computer keyboard and a MIDI pad controller play them, with note repeat, and the piano roll shows a row per chop. One Undo step takes it all away. **To a Slicer instead** puts the same chops on a Slicer.
- **Chop and play** (on by default) writes a 2-bar starting pattern on the Drum Rack track that plays the chops in a new order on the beat grid: the same chop on each downbeat, the others shuffled, a stutter here and there. Off, the clip plays the chops in their own order and time, as the sample did. **New idea** (in the Drum Rack's panel) writes another pattern into the track's selected clip; the same sample always gives the same ideas in the same order, and the pattern is plain notes, so exports play exactly what you hear. Undo goes back to the last idea.
- **Each chop** is a pad: select it for its **Start**, **End** and **Fade** (where in the sample it plays), and the usual **Level**, **Pan**, **Pitch**, **Decay**, filters, **Drive**, **Choke** group and **reverse** (the chop played backwards).
- **Filter out the bass** (in the chop dialog, and on the Audio, Drum Rack and Slicer panels) turns the track's **Filter** insert into a steep low cut at 180 Hz: the sample's bass and kick go (about 22 dB less under 100 Hz) and its chords stay, so your own 808 has the low end to itself. Untick it to turn the filter off.

### Fledge Synth

**Fledge Synth** is Fledgetone's own synthesizer, made for modern electronic music: dubstep, riddim and tearout basses, wobbles, growls and "yoi" talking basses, supersaw leads, future bass chords, keys, pads and risers. Add it with **Add Track → Fledge Synth** (it's also under **Plugins › Browse plugins… › Instruments**).

**Presets and macros** (always at the top):

- **Preset**: 57 sounds in groups (Bass, Lead, Keys, Chords, Pad, FX), with **‹ ›** to step through them. They're all about as loud as each other, so flicking through causes no jump in level. The basses **Talking Growl**, **Riddim Cell**, **Colour Chord Stab**, **Neuro Reese** and **Dive Screech** change with every note (each note picks its own vowel, grit, comb or dive) rather than wobbling at one rate, and come back alike each bar, opening and closing a little over two; they're bright on top, so they cut through the mix. **Clean Sub** is a plain mono sine to lay under them; there's also **Hardcore Supersaw**, **Wide Wash** (a wide, slow pad with some air on top, its low notes kept in the middle) and **Bright Pluck**. For a loud, crunchy modern drop: **Crunch Formant** (a talking growl hard-clipped before its vowel filter), **Resample Chop** (as if resampled and chopped: every 16th a new slice of the sound, crushed), **Clipped Square** (a square under a saw, clipped flat, jumping and gliding across two octaves), **Hard Clip Stab** (one key plays a short fifth-and-octave stab, clipped) and the lead **Chip Crunch** (a narrow, bit-crushed square with a short glide and a slap of echo). Like the other movement basses, each note gets its own sound and they come back alike each bar. **Driven Legato** is the other way round: one gritty, clipped saw-and-square voice that holds through a run of notes, gliding into each one that overlaps the last (draw them with the piano roll's Legato tool), with no new attack and no per-note change, so a riff leaping octaves stays one sound; its brightness sweeps slowly over two bars. Lay **Clean Sub** under it, or keep its own clean sub. For melodic songs (piano-led artcore, future bass): **Soaring Saw** (a wide supersaw lead with a gentle vibrato that comes in on held notes), **Silk Stack** (dark held supersaw chords to sit under a lead), **Silk Wash** (a brighter, further-away Wide Wash), **Round Root** (Driven Legato tamed: a bass that holds a chord's root under piano and pads, no glide, no sub of its own), **Vowel Chop** (short notes like a chopped vocal), **Glass Pluck** (a clear, bell-like pluck, every partial in tune, for doubling a tune high up) and **Reverse Swell** (a bar-long swell like a reversed cymbal that stops dead at the end of the note, for the beat before a drop).
- **Macros**: four knobs that each move several settings at once, named for what they do in the preset (on Wobble: **Speed up**, **Squelch** and **Grit**; on Growl: **Open**, **Growl** and **Grit**). Every Bass preset has three. Under each is what it moves; on your own sound, or a macro you've rerouted, the name is what it moves. Automate a macro to change a sound across a song with one lane.
- **Wobble rate** (or **Pulse rate** for a gate or a pump, **Sweep rate** for a sweep that runs once per note), beside the macros when the sound has an LFO in time with the song: the wobble's speed as a note length (1/4, 1/8, triplets, dotted…), so it stays locked to the tempo. It's the same setting as that LFO's rate on the Modulation tab.
- **Undo** takes back a turn of a knob or a change of preset, like any other edit (a drag is one step).
- **Knobs**: drag up or down (hold Shift for fine steps), or click one and use the arrow keys. Double-click a knob to put it back. Each shows its value under its name.

**Sound** tab:

- **Oscillator A and B**: each plays a **Table** of 32 shapes that **Position** moves through. The 16 tables run from Basic shapes (sine → triangle → saw → square) and Pulse width to the talking and growling ones (Vowels, Growl FM, FM 1:2, Screech), Sync sweep, Organ, Octave stack, Digital, Resonant and Random harmonics. The picture shows the wave as you change it.
  - **Warp** bends the wave: **Sync** (the classic sync sweep), **Bend**, **Squeeze**, **Mirror**, **Quantize** (lo-fi steps), **Fold**, and **Ring** (times the other oscillator), by its **Amount**.
  - **Unison** stacks up to 16 copies, spread by **Detune**; **Blend** moves weight from the centre copy to the detuned ones, and **Width** spreads them across the stereo field. **Stack** tunes the copies in turn up an octave, a fifth or the notes of a chord (with Unison 2 or more).
  - **Octave**, **Semi**, **Fine** and **Pan**; **Phase** and **Random** set where each note starts in the wave (0 Random gives every note the same punch); **On** turns an oscillator off.
  - The menu at the top right sends it to **Filter 1**, **Filter 2**, **Both filters**, or **Straight out** past them.
- **Filter 1** and **Filter 2**: low pass (12, 24 and a ladder), band pass, high pass, notch, **Comb** (metallic, pitched to the note) and **Formant** (vowel sounds as **Cutoff** moves). The two run side by side, or **Filter 1 into 2** one after the other. **Key track** makes a filter follow the note; **Envelope** opens it with the filter envelope.
- **Sub, noise and FM**: a clean sine an octave or two below (kept in the centre), noise (with its own route), and **FM B→A**, where oscillator B bends A for growls and bells. **Sub goes** is *Clean, after effects* on new sounds: the sub joins the mix after the drive, the OTT and the effects, so a growl can be crushed while the sub stays solid and mono. Sounds saved before keep *With the voices*, as they had it.
- **Drive**, in up to three places: **Before the filters**, **After the filters** (with **Cab tone**, a speaker-cabinet-like tone that takes the lows and the fizz off), and **Mids and highs** after the voices (split at **Lows below** and **Highs above**: the lows stay clean; **Highs** sets how much of the highs it drives). Each has soft, warm, hard clip, fold, asymmetric, **Tube**, **Bitcrush** and **Downsample** (with **Crush** for the bits or the rate) and a **Mix**; each is levelled so turning it up adds grit, not volume.
- **Envelopes**: **Volume** and **Filter** attack, decay, sustain and release (each drawn as you set it), and a **Pitch drop** for punchy starts: set **Amount** to +12 st, **Time** to 50 to 150 ms and **Curve** to Linear for a dive into each note.
- **Playing**: **Voices** is Poly (chords), Mono or Legato (notes that overlap slide into each other, with **Glide**, and the same pitch holds on). With **Restart each note** on (as in the new presets), a Mono or Legato note that doesn't overlap the last starts afresh, the last one fading out under it in 3 ms, so a riff that repeats sounds the same each time round; off (as older sounds have it), each note carries on from where the last one's oscillators were. **Glide** can be *Always* or *Legato only* (only between notes that overlap), take a constant time or a **Constant rate** (the Glide time per octave, so small steps are quick), and be exponential or linear. **Bend** sets the pitch bend range, **Velocity** how much louder harder notes play.

**Modulation** tab:

- **LFO 1 to 4**: Sine, Triangle, Saw, Square, **Wobble**, Steps, Drift, **Pump** (the sidechain duck: set a target of Volume at +100%), and **Drawn 1** and **2**. **Sync to song** locks the rate to the tempo (straight, dotted and triplet values from 1/64 to 4 bars), so a wobble stays in time when you change the tempo. **Mode** is **Free** (one LFO for every note, following the song: the same place in the song is the same point of its cycle, live and in an export), **Retrig** (from the top with each note), **Envelope** (once per note, then it holds where it ended) or **Sustain env** (runs to **Hold at** and stays there while the key is down, then finishes once it's let go). For Mono and Legato sounds, **Retrigger** says which notes restart it: as the voices do, **Always**, **Legato continues** (only a note after a gap) or **Never**. **Phase** sets where it starts, **Delay** waits before it starts, **Rise** brings it in slowly, **Smooth** rounds off its corners, and **Key track** runs it faster on higher notes. Each moves up to two **Targets** by its **Amount**, which can go either way.
- **Mod envelope**: a third envelope for anything (the Yoi preset sweeps its vowel with it; into Pitch in the matrix at +100% it's a 12-semitone dive).
- **Chaos and random**: **Chaos** is a source that steps to a new random level at its **Chaos rate** (in time with the song), slid by **Smooth**; **Note random** (0 to 1) and **Note random ±** (either way) pick a new value at each note. They follow the song, so a song plays the same every time and its export matches; **Repeats every** (1 bar at first) brings the same values round again, so a riff that repeats sounds alike each time. The value goes with the step in the bar, not the pitch: the riff played over the next chord's root has each step sound as it did (the notes of a chord each get their own, from the lowest up, whatever order they're played in). **By step, not pitch** is on in new sounds; sounds saved before it came have it off and keep their values by pitch, exactly as they played. Set **Repeats every** to 2 or 4 bars for a riff that varies over a longer stretch, or **Never** for new values every time.
- **Matrix**: 16 slots (9 to 16 open with **Slots 9 to 16**, and show by themselves when used), each a **Source** (the LFOs, the three envelopes, velocity, the note, mod wheel, aftertouch, pitch bend, a random value per note, note random, chaos, release velocity, note pressure, the macros) moving a **Target** (pitch, either filter's cutoff and resonance, table positions, warps, levels, detune, FM, each drive, crush, noise, pan, the LFOs' rates, the effects' mix, width) by an **Amount**, bent by its **Curve** (linear, exponential, logarithmic or S) and scaled by an optional **Via** source (for example velocity, so harder notes move it further). The presets keep their macros in slots 5 to 8.
- **Drawn shapes**: drag across the 16 bars to draw a rhythm or a curve; **Smooth** slides each step into the next. Choose Drawn 1 or 2 as an LFO's shape to use it (gates, stutters, custom wobbles).

**Effects** tab (part of the preset, each with **On**): **EQ** (low, mid and high, each with its frequency), **Chorus**, **Phaser** or **Flanger**, **Delay** (in time with the song or in ms, **Ping-pong**, **Tone**) and **Reverb** (size, decay, tone, pre-delay). **Output** has **OTT** (the squashed, up-front sound of modern bass music), **Width**, **Mono below** and **Level**. Everything under **Mono below** stays in the middle, whatever the unison, the chorus and the width do, so the bass stays solid and a wide chord with low notes doesn't smear the low end: 180 Hz in new sounds (250 Hz in Wide Wash), 120 Hz in older ones. A safety limiter keeps every note under −1 dB.

Every knob can be automated (a lane per setting, like any plugin), learned to a MIDI controller, frozen, bounced and exported, and the settings are saved with the song. On a phone, Pocket plays Fledge Synth too, with its presets, its macros as big sliders and the wobble rate in its **Sound** tool (see [Fledgetone Pocket: on a phone](#fledgetone-pocket-on-a-phone)). It's light on the computer: an 8-note chord on the heaviest presets takes about a tenth of one core.

### Plugins

The **Plugins** menu holds hundreds of built-in effects and instruments: Fledge Synth (above), open-source plugins from Airwindows, MDA, Mutable Instruments, Faust and Open303, plus the Surge XT synth, the Dragonfly and Plateau reverbs and the setBfree tonewheel organ, compiled to run in the browser (see Credits for who made them). They need no address and nothing to allow.

- **Plugins › Browse plugins…** opens the plugin browser: search by name or by what it does ("tape", "warm", "bell", "granular"), or pick a group down the side: **Instruments**, **Recommended** and **Basic** (the Airwindows picks), then each kind of effect (Reverb, Saturation, Dynamics, Filter, Lo-Fi and more). **Effects go on** chooses the track. **Add** puts an effect on that track; an instrument's **New track** makes a track that plays it. The menu also has shortcuts straight to the reverbs, delays, saturation, tape, dynamics, filters and others.
- **Effects** go after the track's instrument and before its own inserts, in the order you add them, like the web plugins below. Right-click a track's name for **Add plugin effect…**, and to open or remove each one.
- **Instruments** become tracks you write notes for, record onto and play from a MIDI keyboard, like any other: an acid bass line synth; electric piano, piano, FM and analog poly synths; 22 synthesis engines (analog, FM, wavetable, chords, speech, swarm, drums and more); resonators, modal voices, bells and physical models of brass, woodwinds, strings, guitars and percussion.
- **Its window** opens when you add it, with what it does and a slider for each setting, showing the value as the plugin does. Double-click a slider to put it back; **Reset all** puts them all back. Plugins that come with presets have a **Preset** list at the top.
- **★** a plugin in the browser to keep it under **Favourites**, and on the Plugins menu (added to the selected track in one click). **Recently used** lists the last ones you added.
- Everything works as for any plugin: automation lanes for every setting, MIDI learn, freezing, bouncing, exports, and the settings saved with the project.
- **Effects that make their own sound** (Shepard's tones, the noise sources, click tracks) play while their track plays: a few seconds after the track goes quiet they fade out, and they come back with its next sound, so they can't drone on through the song or while it's stopped. Reverb and delay tails fade by themselves as usual; **Infinity** and **Infinity2**, made to hold a sound forever, still do.
- The plugins download the first time you open the browser or use one (about 9 MB in all, in a few files) and are kept in this browser. **Master tab › Instruments offline › Download all** includes them, for working offline.

### Plugins from the web (Web Audio Modules)

The app can also load **Web Audio Modules** (WAM 2): instruments and effects other people make for the web, each at its own address. Look for them in the WAM community's plugin lists; you need the address of the plugin's module (usually ending `index.js`), and its site has to let other sites load it.

- **A plugin instrument**: **Add Track → Plugin instrument (WAM)…**, paste the address and press **Load plugin**. It becomes a track you write notes for, record onto and play from a MIDI keyboard like any other (notes, velocity, held keys, pitch bend and the expression lanes go to it as MIDI).
- **A plugin effect**: right-click a track's name (any but a bus), **Add plugin from a web address (WAM)…** (or **Plugins › Effect from a web address (WAM)…**). Effects come after the track's instrument and before its own inserts, in the order you add them. Right-click the track to open or remove each one.
- **Its window**: the plugin's own controls open when you add it. Reopen them with the plug icon on the track's header, or from the right-click menu. Drag the window by its title bar; **BYPASS** (effects) lets the sound pass it by; closing the window leaves the plugin playing.
- **Its settings** are read from it every few seconds and when its window closes, and saved with the project, so exports, freezing and reopening the project hear it as you set it.
- Addresses you've used are listed under **Used before** in the dialog.

Plugins are programs from their sites, so only load ones you trust. A project you open that uses plugins plays without them at first, and a bar at the top names the sites: **Load plugins** allows them (remembered in this browser). Plugins need an internet connection (the site they come from), and a plugin that fails to load says why and stays silent (an effect lets the sound through). A plugin is only as good as its maker made it: one that's slow can make playback stutter, and freezing its track helps.

### Where your sounds live

Sound files (audio clips, the Slicer and the Sampler) are kept in this browser, once each, shared between tracks and projects, so a big file doesn't fill up autosave. **Save** puts the sounds inside the `.fledgetone.json` file, so a saved project opens complete on any computer, and opening it puts the sounds back into the browser. The Audio, Slicer and Sampler panels show how much the browser holds, and **Free unused** deletes the sounds no project on screen, restorable session or kept version (**File › Versions…**) uses. A clip whose sound was freed shows "Sound not in this browser" until you load the sound again or reopen the project file.

## Mixing and effects

### The Mixer

The **Mixer** tab of the bottom panel shows every track side by side as a mixing desk: a level meter (after the fader: green, amber near the top, red at the very top), a fader, pan, the reverb (**REV**) and delay (**DLY**) sends, **M**ute and **S**olo, and which inserts are on. The master fader is on the right. These are the same settings as the track headers and the Inspector; each drag is one Undo step and a double-click resets. Click a track's name to open it in the Inspector for its inserts, EQ and automation.

**Make space** (under the strips) carves room in the mix the way a mix engineer does, with small moves on each track's Pro EQ: every part above the bass loses the rumble under its lowest note (a low cut), parts reaching down into the bass's octaves are thinned there by 2 dB, and parts playing in the lead melody's range give way 2.5 dB where the lead's presence is, so the melody comes through without being turned up. It works out the bass, the lead and the rest from the notes. A band you've already moved is left alone; drums, sound effects, audio and buses aren't touched. One Undo takes it all back.

**Glue** (the switch under Make space) is for when a song sounds like instruments playing side by side rather than one record. Switched on, it makes space as above, puts the drums on a drum bus and the other music parts on a music bus (each glued by gentle compression, the music bus breathing a little with the kick), ducks the bass under the kick, sends every part a little to the shared reverb so they sit in one room, and spreads the parts left in the middle a little apart (never the lead, the bass or the drums). Switch it off at any time, even after saving and reopening the song, and everything it changed goes back: only what's still as Glue left it, so a pan or EQ you've changed by hand since stays yours. Its buses go too. Undo works on it as well.

**Duck to kick** (under Glue) makes the whole song breathe with the drums, as dance music does, in the style picked beside it (it starts on the style the **Does it hit?** check is set to). The bass parts get a **Hole for drums** on the kick *and* the snare, so both punch through the low end; the chords, pads and leads pump on the kick and are back within 130 to 230 ms at the song's tempo:

| Style | Bass and sub (kick and snare) | Chords, pads and leads (kick) |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Pumping** (house, darksynth) | 14 dB hole, 150 ms | 6 dB, back in half a beat |
| **Bass music** (dubstep, drumstep) | 18 dB hole, 150 ms | 5 dB, back in 0.45 of a beat |
| **Future bass / artcore** | 12 dB hole, 140 ms | 2 dB, back in 0.4 of a beat |

Those are for drums with room between the hits. When the kicks and snares come half a beat apart or closer (a syncopated drop, say, with six or more kicks a bar), full holes would chop the bass up ten times a bar, so the holes get shallower and shorter the denser the drums: at that density about 5 dB, over within 60 % of a 16th, and the chords, pads and leads pump only about 2.5 dB, back within a 16th. With a sub part of its own (a Sub-Bass, a track named *sub*, or Fledge Synth's **Clean Sub**) the sub takes the hole and the bass over it gets a hole a third as deep, since the room the kick needs is in the lows.

Drums, vocals and sound effects are left alone. It needs a drum track; one Undo takes it all back, and each track's Pump stays yours to change. **Glue** gives the bass a 12 dB hole on the kick and snare too (if it has no Pump yet), and its music bus pumps on the kick only when the parts on it don't pump already. On the bass-music demo drop, the sub went from 3.7 dB under the snare to 21 dB under it, the chords still pumping about 5 dB.

**Spectrum**: right of the master fader, the live spectrum of everything you hear, drawn as a neon display: magenta for the lows, through purple and cyan, to yellow for the highs, with scanlines, a readout of the loudest frequency and the level, and a glitch on hard bass hits. Pick its style from the menu above it, and it's remembered:
- **LED bars**: segmented bars (lows on the left) with peaks that fall slowly.
- **Neon city**: a rainy skyline. Each tower rises with its part of the sound and more of its windows light up, reflected in the wet street.
- **Grid terrain**: the spectrum scrolls toward you as a wireframe landscape (lows in the middle, highs out to the sides).
- **Radar HUD**: the spectrum round a sweeping radar (lows at the top, highs at the bottom), with blips where it's loud, the level in the middle and, when there's room, low/mid/high meters and readouts.
- **Data rain**: streams of glyphs that fall faster, longer and brighter where the sound is loud, with a line tracing the spectrum.
- **Holo pigeons**: the workshop's two pigeons as holograms on projector pads, one faceted and one in pixels. They bob their heads on the beat and flicker.

### Track headers

**Right-click a track's name** for its **colour**, **Move up** / **Move down**, **New folder…**, **Move into** a folder or **Take out of** one, its **Output** (the master, or **Play into** a bus), **Send to a new bus** (or **Group N tracks into a new bus** with clips selected on several tracks), **Send to** each return or **Stop sending**, **Send to a new return**, plugin effects (**Add plugin effect…** from the built-in plugins, **Add plugin from a web address (WAM)…**, and **Open** or **Remove** each one), **Freeze**, **Bounce in place** and **Delete track**.

**Folders**: a folder groups tracks under a row of its own. Its arrow **folds** them away (they still play, and the folder's row shows their clips in miniature, so you can see where the music is); double-click the folder's lane to fold or unfold too. Its **M** and **S** mute or solo every track in it, the folder icon sets a colour for it and its tracks, double-click its name to rename it, and **×** ungroups it (the tracks stay where they are). Tracks in a folder show a thin stripe of its colour. Moving a track up or down past the edge of its folder takes it out; a track outside folders hops over a whole folder. Folders are saved with the project and every change is one Undo step (folding isn't).

Each track header has **VOL** (-60 to +6 dB) and **PAN** sliders (double-click either to reset), and buttons for **M** (mute), **S** (solo) and **R** (record arm: takes go on this track, one track at a time; arming also selects it). If any track is soloed, only soloed tracks play. Muted and un-soloed tracks are also left out of exports.

Tracks are stereo, so wide sounds keep their width all the way to the master: the Supersaw, Space Clav and Mandolin, the noise in the FX instrument, the **Chorus** effect, recorded instruments with stereo samples, and stereo files in the Sampler. Most synths and mono samples are mono and sit in the middle, and so are the drum kits, effects included (a Chorus on a kit doesn't widen it). **PAN** works as a balance: in the middle both sides play, and turning it towards one side fades out the other, so at full left you hear only the left channel. For a mono sound that's the same as moving it across.

### Buses (group channels)

A **bus** is a track with no clips of its own that other tracks play into, so they share one fader, one set of inserts and EQ, and one pair of sends: put all the drums through one compressor, or turn every backing part down together.

- **Make one**: **Add Track → Bus (group channel)**, or select clips on each of the tracks you want, right-click one of those tracks' names and choose **Group N tracks into a new bus** (for just the track you right-click, **Send to a new bus**).
- **Route a track**: right-click its name and pick **Play into “Bus name”**, or **Output: back to the master**; or use the output menu at the bottom of its Mixer strip. The track's header shows **→ Bus name**, and the bus shows **bus · N** (how many tracks play into it).
- A bus has a fader, pan, every insert and the EQ, sends, mute, solo, automation and a meter, like any track. At 0 dB, centred and with nothing on, it leaves the sound exactly as it was.
- **Mute** a bus and its tracks go silent; **solo** it and you hear it with everything playing into it. Soloing a track in a bus keeps the bus playing.
- Buses can play into other buses (a drums bus into a music bus). A route that would come back round to itself isn't offered.
- Deleting a bus sends its tracks back to the master. Buses can't hold clips, be armed or be frozen. Exports, stems and freezing play through them as you hear them.
- **Bus presets** set a bus up for its job in one click. Select a bus and pick one under **Bus preset** in the Inspector, or use **Track › Bus presets** to gather the right tracks (those playing straight to the master) into a new bus with it:
  - **Drum bus**: glue compression and a little saturation, so the kit hits as one.
  - **808 / bass bus**: **Mono bass** keeps everything under 120 Hz in the middle, saturation adds harmonics small speakers can play, and gentle compression evens the notes.
  - **Vocal bus**: a low cut, a little less boxiness at 300 Hz and more air on the Pro EQ, compression, and a de-esser (the multiband compressor squeezing only the "s" range, over 5.5 kHz).
  - **Music bus**: a low cut at 80 Hz that leaves the bottom to the bass, a gentle dip in the mud and light glue.
  A preset only sets the effects it uses; change any of them afterwards. One Undo takes it back.
- Two inserts made for buses show on every bus (and on any track where they're on): **Saturation** rounds off the peaks like tape or a warm desk, thickening and gluing without losing the lows (unlike the Distortion, which tightens the bass); **Drive** is how hard and **Mix** how much. **Mono bass** puts everything under its **Crossover** (40 to 300 Hz) in the middle.

### Return tracks (effect returns)

A **return** is a track for a shared effect: put a reverb, a delay or any effect on it, and send as much of any track to it as you like. Many tracks share one room or one echo, and each decides how much of itself goes in, without changing its own sound.

- **Make one**: **Add Track → Return (effect return)**, or right-click a track's name and choose **Send to a new return** (that track starts sending to it at 50%).
- **Put effects on it**: select the return and turn on its inserts in the Inspector, usually the **Reverb** or **Delay** (on a return they start at 100% mix, since the dry sound already plays from the tracks), or any plugin effect.
- **Send to it**: every track's Inspector has a slider for each return under the sends (and **+ Send to a new return**), each track's Mixer strip has one too, and the track menu has **Send to “Return name”** and **Stop sending**. **pre** takes the send before the track's fader, so the return keeps going as the track fades (or is turned all the way down); otherwise the send follows the fader.
- A return has a fader, pan, every insert, plugin effects, mute and automation like any track, and can play into a bus. It shows **return · N** (how many tracks send to it). Returns are solo-safe: soloing a track keeps the returns playing, so you hear it with its reverb.
- Send levels can be automated (pick **Send → Return name** in a track's automation LANE). Deleting a return removes the sends to it (Undo brings them back). Exports, stems and freezing play through returns as you hear them; a frozen track still feeds its returns live.

### The Track Inspector (FX Rack)

Click **FX Rack** in the toolbar, or the sliders icon on a track header (or **Track › Inspector & FX rack**), to open the Inspector on the right. Click another track's header to switch to it. The **Track FX** tab, top to bottom:

- **SOUND GENERATOR**: the track's instrument. Changing it renames and recolours the track but keeps its clips. Under it, the instrument's own panel where it has one (the Synth Designer, the 808 designer, the Drum Designer, the Sampler, Drum Rack, Multi Sampler, Slicer and Audio panels, an Audio track's **Vocal chain**, a bus's **Bus preset**), **Glide**, **Sub enhancer** and the kick and bass check on the bass instruments (see Heavy bass tools), and **Expressive playing** on the recorded instruments and drum kits.
- **HUMAN**: makes the track sound played rather than programmed. A real player never hits the same note quite the same way twice, so with Human on every note comes out a little different: **Timing** puts notes a few milliseconds either side of the beat (up to ±8 ms), **Dynamics** plays them a little softer or harder (up to ±30%), and **Pitch** leaves them a hair sharp or flat (up to ±12 cents, like a string player or a slightly loose guitar; only on instruments that play pitches, so drum kits don't show it). The notes of a chord land a hair apart, like fingers. Each note's variation is worked out from the note itself, so the song sounds the same every time you play it and in your exports, while no two notes are alike. Your notes aren't changed: switch it off and they play exactly as written. Start around 30 to 50% for a natural feel; turn Timing right up for a loose, laid-back one. (The piano roll's **Humanize** tool is different: it moves the notes themselves, once.) A new track on a recorded instrument starts with Human on at a player's feel for it: an orchestra's players a little loose in time and touch, a band's a little less, keyboards and mallets tighter, a drum kit tight in time with only its hits' strength varying. Synths start exact, as a machine plays.
- **DUCK OTHERS UNDER THIS**: for a track that stands in for the game's dialogue or sound effects (a voice line, placeholder effects). Switch it on and every other track dips by **Dip** (2 to 24 dB, 8 to start) wherever this track sounds: a moment before each note or audio clip (so the first syllable isn't masked), holding while it sounds, and back up 0.4 s after; gaps under about a third of a second are bridged. It follows the track's clips as you edit them, plays live and in every export, and isn't written as automation. Muting the proxy stops the ducking. The Mixer marks a proxy track **PROXY**. (To dip under a reference video's own dialogue instead, use the speech bubble on the video lane.)
- **DISTORTION**: Drive, from clean at 0% through overdrive to fuzz at 100%. It's a pedal-style overdrive: the lows are tightened, the top is rolled off the harder you drive, and the level stays about the same, so turning it up changes the tone rather than the volume.
- **BASS DRIVE**: distortion on the mids and highs only, the sub kept clean and mono (see Heavy bass tools).
- **3-BAND EQ**: three bands split at 400 Hz and 2.5 kHz: **LOW** (below 400 Hz), **MID** (400 Hz to 2.5 kHz) and **HIGH** (above 2.5 kHz), each -24 to +12 dB. At 0 dB the bands add back up to exactly the sound that went in. **Reset** flattens them.
- **PRO EQ**: a parametric EQ you draw. Up to six bands, each a **Low cut**, **Low shelf**, **Bell**, **Notch**, **High shelf** or **High cut**. Drag a numbered dot sideways for its frequency and up or down for its gain (Shift: fine); the mouse wheel over the graph changes the selected band's width (Q); double-click empty space to add a band there; double-click a dot to switch it off or on; right-click a dot to remove it. Under the graph: the selected band's shape, frequency, gain and Q. The curve is exactly what the filters do, and while the song plays the track's own sound is drawn behind it, so you can see what you're cutting. **Open big** shows it in the **EQ & Dynamics** tab of the bottom panel. It starts flat, with a low shelf, two bells and a high shelf ready to drag (and a low cut and high cut, off).
- **COMPRESSOR**: Threshold, Ratio and Makeup gain.
- **CHORUS**: Rate, Depth and Mix.
- **MULTIBAND**: a compressor in three bands, **Low**, **Mid** and **High**, split at **Low / mid** and **Mid / high** (the bands add back up exactly, so with nothing squeezing the sound is unchanged). Each band has its own **Threshold**, **Ratio** and **Gain**, or **bypass**; **Attack** and **Release** are shared. Meters show how much each band is turning down. Tame a boomy low end without dulling the top, or squash a harsh top only when it gets loud. (The Master's **OTT** is a ready-made multiband for the whole mix.)
- **SATURATION** and **MONO BASS**: on buses (and on any track where they're on); see Buses.
- **LO-FI**: **Crush** rounds the sound to fewer bits (16-bit is clean, 3-bit a buzzy 8-bit crunch), **Tone** darkens the top (1 to 20 kHz), **Wow** wobbles the pitch slowly like a worn tape, and **Mix** blends it with the clean sound. For lo-fi keys, dusty drums, chiptune grit and cowbell beats.
- **FILTER**: **Low-pass**, **High-pass** or **Band-pass**; **Cutoff** (20 Hz to 20 kHz); **Resonance** (Q 0.5 to 15: high values boost a narrow band at the cutoff, up to about +23 dB, so turn the track down when you push it); **Slope** 12 or 24 dB. It comes after the other effects, so it sweeps everything on the track. Moving Cutoff or Resonance while playing glides smoothly.
- **DELAY**: echoes in time with the song. **Time** is a note value (1/32 to 1/2, dotted and triplet ones too) of the tempo; **ping-pong** bounces the echoes left and right; **Feedback** (how many repeats), **Tone** (darkens each repeat) and **Mix**.
- **REVERB**: a room of the track's own (apart from the shared master reverb): **Decay** (0.3 s for a small room to 12 s for a huge hall), **Pre-delay** (a gap before the room answers, which keeps a voice clear), **Tone** (darker is warmer) and **Mix**. On a return track, run it at 100% mix.
- **GATE**: chops the track's volume in time with the song (a trance gate, or a stutter before a drop). **Rate** 1/8, 1/8T, 1/16, 1/16T or 1/32; **Depth** (how far it closes: 100% is silent between chops); **Length** (how much of each step it stays open). The chops line up with bar 1, in playback and exports alike. Try it on a held pad or supersaw chord.
- **PUMP**: sidechain-style ducking. The track's volume dips and swells back on what **TRIGGER** is set to: every **Kick** (any drum-kit track's kick that is actually playing), every **Snare** (its snares and claps; a recorded kit's rimshot counts as a snare; a percussion kit's rows, such as Latin Percussion's tumba, conga and claves, aren't kicks or snares and don't trigger it), **Kick+snare** (both: a hole in the bass for every drum hit), every **Beat**, every **1/8**, or every note of a **Track** you pick under **Follows** (a sidechain from any part: duck the pads under the bass, or the chords under a lead; the followed track can even be muted, as a silent trigger). The **shape** is how it dips and comes back, drawn under the buttons: **Smooth** (down at once, then a smooth swell back: the classic pump, and how every Pump sounded before there were shapes), **Hard cut** (cut right down, held, then straight back), **Short tick** (a quick dip that's back almost at once), **Long swell** (creeps back over the whole length) and **Hole for drums** (down within about 20 ms, held while the drum hits, back by about 150 ms: for basses under the kick and snare). Drag a point of the curve to draw your own (**Drawn**), click the curve to add a point, double-click one to take it away; it always starts and ends at full volume, and no edge is quicker than 3 ms (even Hard cut at a short Length, or two points on top of each other), so it never clicks; each drag is one undo step. **Depth** (how far it ducks, shown in dB) and **Release** (50 to 600 ms, how long it takes to come back; with a shape other than Smooth it's called **Length**, the whole duck's). A muted or soloed-out kick track doesn't pump, so if you solo the pumped track, solo the drums too. It plays the same live and in exports.
- **POST-FADER AUX SENDS**: how much of the track goes to the shared reverb and the ping-pong echo, and to each **return track** (see Return tracks), each before or after the fader. A new track starts with the sends its kind of part usually has: pads and chords about 22% to the reverb, leads 18% and their own **Delay** insert on at a dotted eighth, low in the mix; drums and basses dry; anything else 10%. The shared reverb's send goes up to **200%** for a very wet sound; click **300%** beside it to let it go to 300% (drenched, for washes and effects). An automation lane for the reverb send runs from 0 to 100%.
- **CHANNEL FADER**: the same volume and pan as the header, and **DIST**, which sets how far back on the stage the track sits, from **Front** (as recorded, up close) to 100% (the back of the hall). Further back, a part is a little darker, a little quieter and more of it reaches the reverb, as a far instrument sounds in a real room. New orchestral tracks take their usual seat in an orchestra, seen from the audience: first violins to the left, cellos and basses to the right, woodwinds in the middle behind the strings, horns behind them on the left, trumpets, trombones and tuba to the right at the back, timpani at the very back (double-click DIST to put a track back in its seat). Band instruments, synths and your own sounds start up front.
- **Delete this track** (also the trash icon at the top). It asks first if the track has clips; `Ctrl+Z` brings the track and its clips back.

Human, Duck others under this, Distortion, Bass Drive, Pro EQ, Compressor, Chorus, Multiband, Saturation, Mono bass, Lo-fi, Filter, Delay, Reverb, Gate and Pump each have an **ON / OFF** switch and start off (except Human on a new recorded-instrument track, above). An effect that's off is taken out of the sound completely, so it costs nothing. You can switch effects on and off while the song plays without a gap, a click or a doubled sound, however busy the computer is: the change crossfades over 10 ms, about 20 to 30 ms after the click. A Compressor switched on comes in about 0.2 s later, once it has settled, and any other switch on that track in the meantime waits for it. A compressor holds the sound back by a few milliseconds (it looks ahead), so while any track, bus or return has a Compressor or Multiband on (or plays an electric guitar amp, or has Bass Drive on, which holds its track back 4 ms), the other tracks are held back by the same amount and everything stays in time: a compressed kick still lands with the bass. When that amount changes while the song plays, the other tracks slide to it over about a second, too gently to hear, so a held bass note doesn't dip. Playback then runs those few milliseconds later, recording allows for it, and exports start exactly on time. Turning a control while the song plays glides to the new value in a few milliseconds, so mute, solo and dragged sliders don't click. The EQ is on whenever any band isn't at 0 dB. Double-click any insert's slider to reset it to its starting value (EQ bands go back to 0 dB). The send sliders and the Master Reverb and Ping-Pong Echo sliders don't reset on double-click (the master OTT, Stereo Width and Level sliders do).

### Master

The Inspector's second tab, **Master**, works on the whole mix. At the top:

- **Loudness**: how loud the song is, measured the way streaming services measure it (LUFS). **Now (3 s)** is the last three seconds, **Since Play** the whole stretch since you last pressed Play, and **Peak** the highest true peak (dBTP). A line under them says what the level means: quiet, about streaming level (around -14 LUFS), loud like a club master, or very loud. The peak turns red above -1 dBTP, where a song can distort once a streaming service converts it. **Reset** starts measuring again. The header shows the **Now** reading too, from the moment audio is running, and turns red with the peak.
- **Glue**: the master compressor that holds the whole mix together, in five styles. **Standard** (how every song sounded before there was a choice), **Gentle** (barely touches it: acoustic, orchestral, jazz), **Punchy** (a slower attack lets each drum hit through before it holds the rest: rock, hip-hop, pop), **Tight** (firm and loud: dubstep, EDM, drum and bass) or **Off**. The starters pick one for their genre.
- **OTT**: the multiband compressor of bass music. It splits the mix into lows, mids and highs, turns each band's loud parts down and its quiet parts up, and adds back the level it takes, so the mix gets denser, brighter and louder. **Depth** is how much (start around 20 to 40%), **Time** how fast it reacts (snappy to slow), **In gain** drives it harder and **Out gain** turns the result up or down.
- **Stereo Width**: makes the mix wider (up to 200%) or narrower (0% is mono). **Keep the bass in the middle** (on by default) leaves everything below about 120 Hz centred however wide the rest goes, so the low end stays strong on club systems and phones.
- **Mono below 120 Hz**: everything under 120 Hz in the middle whether the Width is on or not (a wide pad's low notes, a stereo bass). On for new songs; songs made before it keep their sound until you tick it. The Loud master always has it on.
- **Level**: turns the whole mix up or down (-24 to +12 dB) after the OTT and Stereo Width, just before the limiter. Turning it up pushes the mix into the limiter, which is how you make a master louder; the limiter keeps the peaks down. It works whether the OTT is on or off. Double-click it to go back to 0 dB.
- **Loud master**: makes the song as loud as its style for an export, without blasting your ears while you work. The lows go in the middle (Mono below 120 Hz), an optional **Sub tilt** (a low shelf at 90 Hz, -4 to +4 dB) gives more or less sub, then the mix is pushed into a soft, oversampled clipper (the Clipper's own settings if it's on) that shaves the drum and sub peaks, and the limiter holds the rest under -1 dBTP. **Target** is how loud: **Streaming** (-14 LUFS), **Loud** (-9 LUFS: future bass, house, pop EDM) or **Club** (-6 LUFS: dubstep, drumstep, hardcore). **Match** (and switching it on, or a new target) plays the song through the master faster than real time and finds the **Push** (the gain into the clipper and limiter) that gets there, measuring and adjusting a few times; you then hear what will export (level-matched: see Playback). Every export with it on finds the push afresh, so the file lands within about half a LU of the target; an export of the whole song at the Loud master's own target also keeps that push for what you hear (a loop, a version without drums or another target leaves it as it was). It won't push past what's clean: once the clipper and limiter would change the waveform by more than about 9% (-21 dB of distortion, past which the mids crackle), it stops short and says how far. On the bass-music demo drop, Club landed at -7.0 LUFS (peaks about 6.6 dB over the body, as finished drops are); a future-bass drop at Loud reached -9 LUFS with hardly any clipping. **Crunch** (0 to 100%) is for going further on purpose: the clipper's knee goes hard (flat at 100%), and the push may change the waveform by up to about 35% (-9 dB of distortion), so a dense drop reaches Club loudness and the clipping is heard as crunch on the kick, bass and snare. With Crunch the whole sound goes through the clipper oversampled and is clipped twice, a little under the limiter's ceiling, so even bright hats and crunchy snares driven hard come out with their peaks (between samples too) near where it clips: the limiter after it has next to nothing to do, and the mix, the bass with it, doesn't pump with the drums. (Before, the limiter pulled the whole mix down 2 to 5 dB after every kick and snare of a busy drop.) It gets as loud with a few dB less push, so a song with Crunch may now reach its target where it stopped short before; for a steadier drop with less clipping, turn Crunch down. Moving it matches again once you let go. The -1 dBTP ceiling stays, what you hear stays level-matched, and an MP3 or Ogg export keeps its peaks 1 dB lower with any Crunch, since clipped waves overshoot more once encoded. Off for every song until you switch it on.
- **Clipper**: shaves the tallest peaks (mostly drum hits) flat just before the limiter, the way loud rap and electronic masters get their level: the limiter then has less to do, so the mix gets louder with less squashing. **Drive** pushes more of the mix into it (0 to +18 dB), **Character** goes from **Hard** (a flat top: loudest, most bite) to **Soft** (bends from 6 dB under the ceiling: rounder, warmer), and **Ceiling** is where it clips (-12 to 0 dBFS). It's oversampled four times, so the clipping stays clean instead of fizzy. While the song plays, its meter shows how many dB it's taking off the peaks and how often; past about 3 to 4 dB it starts to sound crunchy. It sounds the same in exports.
- **Reference**: load a finished song you'd like yours to sound like (**Load a reference song**; it stays in this browser for next time, and is never saved in the project or exported). **Measure mix** renders your song (up to two minutes) to measure its loudness and tonal balance; the reference then plays at exactly your mix's loudness, so you compare the sound, not the level (measure again after changing the mix). Press Play and the big **A / B** button flips between your mix and the reference, at the same point in the song, without a gap; the Loudness meter and spectrum show whichever you hear. Under it, a spectrum shows both (your mix solid, the reference dashed), smoothed, and a line says where yours has more or less: the sub, bass, low mids, mids, upper mids, presence or air.
- **Mix check**: **Check the mix** renders the song and each track (up to two minutes) and tells you, in plain words, what to look at, with buttons that select the track to blame: bass that isn't in the middle (sound under about 120 Hz that differs left and right), the kick and the bass fighting (the same low notes, as loud, at the same time: a Pump on the bass following the kick fixes most), muddy low mids (too much 200 to 500 Hz), harsh upper mids (too much 2 to 5 kHz), parts covering each other (two parts in the same range at the same level), a level too quiet or too loud for where the song is going (**Going to** picks the same loudness targets as the Export dialog), and peaks hitting the limiter or the clipper hard. Problems are marked red, things worth a look amber; a clean mix gets a green "nothing to flag". It was tuned on the starter songs, which it passes, and on the same songs spoiled on purpose.
- **Does it hit?** (under the Mix check): holds the loop, or the song (up to 64 bars), against finished songs of a **Style**: **Bass music / dubstep**, **Drumstep**, **J-core / hardcore**, **Darksynth / pumping** or **Future bass / artcore**. It renders the stretch (and, for how the bass moves, the bass parts alone, still ducked by the drums as in the mix), measures it off to the side so the page keeps responding, and shows each measurement as a meter with the style's range in green and what to change when it's outside: the **loudness** and how far the peaks stand over the body; the **balance** of sub, low end, low mids, mids, presence and air; whether the sub and the low end are **in the middle** and how wide the mids are; **between the hits** (whether the sound drops out after each drum, how much the level jumps within a 16th, how quiet the quietest part of each beat gets); **bass movement** (whether the bass's tone changes from 16th to 16th, or only wobbles at one steady rate, the 2000s sound, and whether its pattern comes back each bar); the **pump** (how far the chords and pads dip with the beat and come back); and, from the song's drum notes, how far the **kick** and **snare** stand out of the mix and whether the **sub ducks under the snare** (measured on the snares that aren't on a kick; with four on the floor, where every snare lands on one, it says it's not measured). The ranges come from measuring the drops of finished songs in each style.

- **Playback**: **Listening level-matched** (on by default; the header shows **Level-matched** and how many dB it's turning the song up or down, a click opens this panel): you hear every song at about -15 LUFS, however loud its master is, so switching the Loud master to Club doesn't blast you and a quiet mix isn't buried. It follows the song slowly: it never turns up faster than 1 dB a second (a quiet intro comes up gently), turns down faster, and at once if a loud part arrives; in silence it holds still. It changes only what you hear, never an export or the Loudness meter (which shows what the file will measure). Off, you hear the master as loud as it is. Either way a safety limiter at the very end keeps anything from reaching your speakers louder than -1 dBFS. Where the browser can't run it (an address on plain http rather than https, other than this computer's own), the header shows **Not level-matched** instead: nothing is matched or limited, so keep the volume down, and the Loud master's push isn't heard (the export is still loud). It's a setting for this browser, like the buffering. Below it, how this browser buffers live audio. **Responsive** (the default) has the least delay between a key you play and its sound. **Stable** gives the audio a bigger buffer and more time to schedule notes, so busy moments in big songs play through instead of crackling; Play then starts about a quarter of a second later, and live playing has a few hundredths of a second more delay. It's a setting for this browser, not the project, and takes effect after a reload (the panel offers one; your song opens again after it). Below the choice, **Delay from a key to sound** is the delay the browser measures between a note leaving the app and you hearing it (its audio buffer and your sound device), in milliseconds; keys you play, from a MIDI keyboard, the computer keyboard or the screen, start at once, so this is the delay you feel. Some browsers don't report it.
- **Instruments offline**: recordings are kept in this browser once an instrument has downloaded them, so a song opens at once the next time and the instruments you've used work without an internet connection. The panel shows how many are kept. **Download all** fetches every instrument's recordings and the built-in plugins now (about 280 MB, a few minutes on a fast connection) so they all work offline: a bar shows the progress and **Stop** pauses it (what's done is kept; **Download all** carries on from there). **Remove** frees the space; instruments download their recordings again when next used. Under it, **Storage** says whether the browser has agreed to keep Fledgetone's storage (your autosave, sounds and recordings) when the disk gets full, with **Protect** to ask, and how much space Fledgetone uses in this browser (see *Working offline*).

OTT and Stereo Width sit between the master compressor and the limiter, are off in a new project, are saved with it, can be undone, and switch on or off while the song plays without a gap. The Level (0 dB in a new project), Mono below 120 Hz (on in a new project), the Loud master (off) and the Clipper (off in a new project, after the Level) are saved and undone the same way (a Match's push goes with the switch or target that asked for it). All of them are in exports too.

The **limiter** at the very end looks 5 ms ahead and keeps the true peak (including the peaks between samples that a converter or an MP3 encoder rebuilds) under -1 dBTP, live and in every export. It finds those peaks as the Loudness meter does, at 8 times the sample rate, so they read as ffmpeg and other true-peak meters read them. It turns down smoothly before a peak arrives instead of clipping it, and holds its gain through a bass note's cycles, so loud drops stay clean instead of crackling or pumping.

Under them are the two effects every track shares through its sends:

- **Master Reverb**: Room Decay Time, from 0.8 s (tight) to 12 s. Changing it, or the room, while the song plays never cuts off what's already ringing: the new room takes over smoothly, and the long rooms ring all the way down to silence. **Room** picks the kind of space it is: **Concert hall** (warm and wide, its lows ringing a little longer), **Chamber** (a smaller room: close, clear, a quick bloom), **Church** (stone and height: long, dark, slow to bloom), **Studio room** (short, tight and natural, for drums and bands), **Plate** (dense, bright and smooth at once, for vocals and snares) or **Lush** (a smooth, dense tail that shimmers gently, with no hard echoes, for pads, ballads and ambient). Each of the first five is built the way a real room answers: a few distinct early reflections off the nearest walls, then a tail whose highs die sooner than its lows, and different in each ear. **Lush** is made differently: instead of a room's answer worked out in advance, a set of echoes feeding each other makes the tail as it plays, so it takes far less of the computer's power, even with a long decay, and none when nothing is playing: a good pick for long tails on a slower computer. It's about as loud as the concert hall at the same decay, so switching between them keeps the mix in balance, and it fades over without a gap. (If your browser can't run it, the concert hall plays in its place.) The starters pick one for their genre. One click picks a space: **Room** (1 s), **Studio** (1.8 s), **Hall** (3.2 s, for an orchestra), **Cathedral** (7 s, for choir and organ) or **Heaven** (11 s, for angelic pads and ambient). **Use a recorded room…** loads a recording of a real space instead (an impulse response: a WAV of a clap or sweep dying away in a hall, church or studio; free ones are shared online, often under Creative Commons, so credit the recordist as their licence asks). The reverb then sounds like that room, its tail as long as the recording (up to 12 s), and the recording is saved inside the project file. The **×** next to its name goes back to the generated hall.
- **Ping-Pong Echo Delay**: Echo Subdivision (1/4, 1/8, dotted 1/8 or 1/16, locked to the tempo) and Echo Feedback (0 to 80%).

### Tempo, swing and listening volume

In the header:

- **TEMPO**: the song's starting tempo (tempo changes later in the song are on the ruler: see Tempo changes and time signatures). Click the number to type a tempo from 40 to 240 BPM, with up to two decimals (`115.25`); Enter sets it, Esc cancels. `-` and `+` change it by 1 BPM, and the slider moves in whole BPM. **TAP** sets it from your clicks: tap at least twice; it averages your last few taps and starts over if you pause for more than 2.5 seconds.
- **FIT**: sets the tempo so part of the song lasts a given time. Choose **Loop In→Out**, **Whole song** or a number of **Bars**, then type the **Target length** in seconds (`25.5`) or as a timecode (`0:25:00`). It shows the new tempo and the resulting length as you type; **Apply** (or Enter) sets it, to 0.01 BPM, picked so the part ends in the frame you typed. Over long stretches at slow tempos it can land a frame either side, and the panel shows the exact result first.
- **SWING**: delays the off-beat 16th notes (the second 16th of each 8th) for a shuffled feel, from 0 to 50%; at 50% they land on the 1/8-triplet line. Downbeats, 8ths, triplets and 1/32 notes stay on the grid. In an 8th where a clip has a 1/32 roll or triplets, that clip plays the whole 8th straight, so rolls stay even (other clips keep swinging). A clip moved off the 16th grid (Shift-drag, Frame snap) swings on its own 16ths. A clip cut off the grid (the razor with Shift, Snap Off or Frame snap, a start trimmed off the grid, or Insert bars at an off-grid point) keeps the swing it had: its notes go on swinging on the song's 16ths. New blank projects start at 20% (the demo song uses 15%). Double-click it for 0% (straight).
- **LISTEN**: the listening volume (-30 to +6 dB): how loud you hear the song, after the level matching (see Playback in the Master tab). It never changes an export (files are made at 0 dB, with the master as it's set) and isn't saved with the project.

Every track goes through the master compressor (unless Glue is Off) and a limiter that holds peaks just under full scale, so playback never clips and sounds the same as your exports, only level-matched. A safety limiter after the listening volume keeps even +6 dB from clipping your speakers.

**FEEL** (in the piano roll's toolbar) gives one clip its own swing instead of the song's: **Straight**, **Swing 8ths** (light, a triplet shuffle, or a hard dotted lilt: jigs, shuffles, big band) or **Swing 16ths**. It plays the same in playback, exports and the game kit. In a drum clip, **GROOVE…** beside it swings each drum on its own (the hats swung, the kick and snare straight; see the piano roll's helpers).

Mixer and effect changes, tempo changes and time signatures can be undone with `Ctrl+Z`; the starting tempo, swing and listening volume can't.

### When playback lags: the performance badge and Clear memory

While the song plays, the app watches for three signs that the computer isn't keeping up: notes reaching the sound engine too late, the page freezing for a moment, and the sound engine itself falling behind (it can't make the sound as fast as it plays, which you hear as crackles). A few of the first two in a few seconds, or the engine running behind, turns the badge in the header (next to LUFS) into an amber **LAG**, and a warning appears at the top with **Clear memory**. Close the warning with **×** (it stays away until you next press Play). When all is well the badge shows how much memory the page is using (in Chrome).

**Clear memory** (in the warning, or from the badge at any time) lets go of what the app holds but isn't using: downloaded recordings of instruments no track plays, decoded sounds no clip uses, the instrument menu's preview players, and any notes left hanging. Everything comes back by itself if needed (an instrument re-downloads when you use it again). Tick **Also empty the undo history** to free the most on a long session, but then earlier edits can't be undone.

The app also steps in by itself. At the first late note or freeze of a playback, it hands notes to the sound engine further ahead of time (0.4 s instead of 0.1 s) until you press Stop, so the next busy moment is absorbed instead of heard; once a playback has needed that, later ones start with it. The only cost is that a fader, a mute or a jump you make while it plays takes a moment longer to be heard; playing your keyboard or MIDI keyboard isn't affected. And while it lags, the meters and the spectrum draw less often, to leave the computer's time to the music.

If you press Play while instruments are still downloading their recordings (a song just opened), the Play button spins until they're in (ten seconds at most; click it to cancel), so the song doesn't start with them missing.

Big songs also get help without asking. While a song plays, a track with nothing to play for a while rests: it's switched off until just before its next note, so thirty tracks waiting for their entry don't cost what thirty playing ones do (an orchestral test song needed about a third less of the sound engine). Tracks scrolled out of sight in the timeline and the mixer aren't drawn, and the spectrum moves at half speed in songs with more than 16 tracks. The badge's details show **Notes sent ahead: further** while this is on.

Click the badge for the details, including **Audio engine speed** while playing (100% is keeping up). When the engine is what's behind, the warning offers **Use Stable playback** directly; it takes effect after a reload. If playback lags a second time in a session even after notes are sent further ahead, the app switches to Stable playback for next time by itself and tells you so, unless you chose a Playback setting yourself (then yours is kept).

Recordings of instruments no track (stinger or costume) has used for three minutes are let go while the song is stopped, so trying many instruments in a long session doesn't fill the memory; they download again (from this browser's store, at once) if you use them again.

If it still lags: **Freeze** the busiest tracks (below), turn on **Stable playback** (Master tab › Playback), and close other tabs and programs. **Auto-freeze** (below) does most of the freezing for you.

### Freezing tracks

Big songs can ask more of the computer than it has, and playback starts to crackle or skip. **Freezing** a track fixes that: click the snowflake in its header and the app renders the track's instrument, effects and EQ to audio once (a second or two), then plays that recording instead. A frozen track costs about as much as one audio clip: on a 36-track test song, freezing everything cut the playback work to about a quarter.

- A frozen track's snowflake is lit. Its **volume**, **pan** and **sends** (and their automation) stay live, so you can keep mixing it.
- Change anything that went into the recording, such as its notes, instrument, effects, EQ, filter automation, the tempo or the swing, and the freeze is out of date: the snowflake turns amber and the track plays live again, so you always hear the real thing. Click the snowflake to freeze it again. Undo the change and it's frozen again.
- A track's Pump that follows the kick is frozen ducking to the drum tracks that were sounding. Mute those drums, or solo another track, and it plays live until you put them back.
- An armed track plays live while it's armed, so you hear what you play into it.
- Click a lit snowflake to unfreeze. Freezing and unfreezing can be undone.
- Exports always render every track from its instrument, frozen or not, so they sound exactly the same either way.
- The frozen audio stays in this browser (about 10 MB per minute of track in storage, and about 20 MB per minute in memory while the project is open). It isn't saved inside project files: open the project in another browser and frozen tracks play live until you freeze them again. **Free unused** in the sound panels keeps the frozen audio of this project and your last session.
- Audio tracks can't be frozen: they already play recordings.

**Auto-freeze** does this for you in big songs. Once a song has 6 or more instrument tracks, the tracks you haven't changed for a few seconds are frozen in the background while the song is stopped (never while it plays, so nothing changes under you). Their snowflake shows a faint ring. Change one and it plays live again at once, then is frozen again a few seconds after you leave it alone. A track whose plugin window is open plays live and isn't frozen while the window is open, so the plugin's settings are always there to change. On a five-song test mix it cut the playback work by more than half.

- Auto-frozen audio is kept in memory only, never in the project or the browser's storage, and stops at a limit set by the computer's memory (the busiest tracks go first). A track too long to fit is left live. A freeze already under way pauses while the song plays.
- Click a ringed snowflake to freeze that track for good (a normal freeze).
- Turn it off in **Master tab › Playback › Auto-freeze big songs**; it shows how many tracks are frozen and the memory they use. It's a setting for this browser, on by default.

### Bounce in place

Right-click a track's name and choose **Bounce in place** (or **Track ▸ Bounce in place**) to turn it into audio for good: its instrument, inserts and EQ are rendered to one audio clip on a new Audio track right under it. The new track takes over the original's volume, pan, sends, output and their automation, so the mix sounds the same. The original is muted and kept, so you can go back to it. Unlike freezing, the bounce is an ordinary audio clip: cut it, reverse it, warp it, chop it into the Slicer. One Undo step undoes it all.

## Clip launcher

The **Launcher** tab of the dock is a grid of loops: a column for every track and a row for every **scene**. Click a clip to launch it: it starts on the next bar line and loops, in place of the track's arrangement. Launch something else on the same track and it takes over at the next bar line; a pulsing clip is waiting for its turn. This is for jamming, trying sections against each other, and building an arrangement live.

- **Fill the grid**: **Scenes from song** turns each section of the song (between its markers, or every 4 or 8 bars) into a scene. **Send selected clips** (or right-click clips, **Send to launcher**) puts the selected clips into each track's first empty slot. Drag a clip to another slot to move it, Alt-drag to copy it, and hover it for **×** to delete it.
- **Launch a scene** with the ▶ at the start of its row: every track plays its clip in that row, and a track with nothing there stops.
- **Stop** a track with the ■ in its column header (or click an empty slot); **Stop all** stops every clip. **Back to arrangement** lets every track play its arrangement again from the next bar line.
- **Launch on**: launches land on the next beat, half bar, bar (the usual), 2 bars or 4 bars.
- With nothing playing, launching starts playback from the bar the playhead is in. While the launcher is in use the song doesn't loop. Stopping playback ends the session, and the song plays its arrangement again.
- **Write to arrangement** writes what you played, from your first launch to where you stopped, into the timeline (one Undo step): where a track played a slot, copies of its clip; where it was stopped, nothing. So you can jam a song together and then edit it.
- The launcher's clips are saved with the project and undo like everything else. Moving the playhead back before your first launch plays the arrangement until it gets there.

## Automation

Automation makes a track setting change over the song: a filter opening during a build-up, a fade-out, a pan sweep, an echo on one word. You draw a line, and the setting follows it every time the song plays and in every export.

### Opening a lane

Click the **curve button** in a track header ("Show automation"; it's tinted amber when the track has automation). The track's lane switches to **automation view**: its clips are dimmed and can't be edited there, and the header shows **LANE**, the number of points and the value at the playhead. Use **LANE** to pick the setting. Almost anything can be automated:

- **Track**: Volume, Pan, Filter Cutoff, Filter Resonance, Reverb Send, Delay Send, EQ Low, EQ Mid, EQ High.
- **Sends**: the send to each return track.
- **Effects**: these insert settings: Distortion drive, Compressor threshold, ratio and makeup, Chorus rate, depth and mix, Lo-fi tone, wow and mix, Delay feedback, tone and mix, Reverb pre-delay, tone and mix, Gate depth and length, Pump depth and release. A lane for an effect that's off is marked (off); drawing its first point switches the effect on.
- **Plugins**: each parameter of the track's plugins (instrument and effects), named as the plugin names them, over its whole range.

A ● marks settings that already have points. Click the curve button again to go back to the clips.

### Drawing

- Click empty space to add a point (time snaps to the SNAP grid; hold Shift for free time), or click the line to add a point on it.
- Drag a point to move it: time snaps, the value is free, and Shift during the drag stops snapping. Points can't pass their neighbours, but two can share a bar for an instant jump. Alt+drag leaves a copy behind.
- Drag the line between two points to move both up or down.
- **Alt+drag the line** between two points to bend it: up or down pulls its middle that way. A line that starts slow and rushes in at the end makes a build feel like it's taking off; one that falls away fast then settles makes a natural fade. A tooltip shows the bend. Right-click a line for **Bend: slow start**, **Bend: fast start** or **Straighten line**.
- Drag a box on empty space to select points (Shift or Ctrl adds). `Delete` deletes them; `Esc` clears the selection. Double-click or right-click a point to delete just that one.
- **Right-click the lane** for **Ramp up In → Out** and **Ramp down In → Out** (a one-click build-up or fade over the loop region), **Delete selected points**, **Clear lane** and **Remove lane**. Ramps go from silence to the fader level for Volume, 200 Hz to 20 kHz for Filter Cutoff, -24 dB to 0 dB for EQ, and over each setting's usual range for the others (Compressor threshold 0 to -40 dB, for example).
- Every gesture is one undo step.

### How it plays

- Lanes run between their points in straight lines (or bent ones, as drawn); before the first point and after the last they hold that point's value. Volume works like a mixing fader (-60 dB silent to +6 dB; 0 dB is about 84% up); Cutoff (20 Hz to 20 kHz) and Resonance (Q 0.5 to 15) are logarithmic; Pan runs L100 to R100; sends 0 to 100%; EQ -24 to +12 dB.
- While a lane has points it owns that setting: its control shows **AUTO**, follows the lane and can't be moved (click **AUTO** to open the lane). Clear or remove the lane to get the control back.
- Filter lanes only sound while the track's **Filter** is on. Drawing a Filter lane's first point turns it on; if you switch the Filter off later, the lane is kept, dimmed, with a **Turn filter on** button.
- Exports (mixdown and stems) follow lanes to within a millisecond. While playing, an edit, seek or undo is heard on the new line within about a tenth of a second; jumps glide over 4 ms so they don't click.
- Insert and Delete bars on the ruler move points with the music, deleting a track deletes its lanes (Undo brings them back), and lanes are saved with the project.

### MIDI learn: knobs and faders, and writing automation

Any setting a lane can drive can also be moved by a knob or fader on a MIDI controller:

1. Open the track's automation and pick the setting in **LANE**.
2. Click **MIDI** beside it (it pulses), then move a knob or fader on your controller. The button now shows its number (**CC74** and so on).
3. Moving that knob now moves the setting, over its whole range. Each burst of movement is one Undo step.

Right-click the button to forget the knob (or use the list in the MIDI popover, which shows every mapping). A knob moves one setting; learning it for another moves it there. Mappings are saved with the project.

**Write automation**: switch on **W** (beside the MIDI button, or **Write automation from knobs while playing** in the MIDI popover). While the song plays, moving a mapped knob writes its setting's lane at the playhead, replacing what the lane had there, so you can perform a filter sweep or a fade and keep it. Stop playing (or switch W off) to just move the setting again.

## Projects: saving and opening

The project's name, **Save**, **Export**, **Share** and **Game** sit at the right of the menu bar; **New** and **Open** are in the **File** menu.

- **Project name**: click it to rename. It's used for the file names when you save and export.
- **New** (in the **File** menu) replaces what's on screen and clears undo (the song it replaces is offered back by the Restore banner). It offers:
  - **Blank timeline**: four empty tracks.
  - **Starters**: eight songs to start from, all chill and world music: **Lo-Fi** (82 BPM, electric piano, a dusty breakbeat, upright bass, flute and rain), **Ambient** (70 BPM, warm and glass pads, kalimba, crystal sparkles), **Celtic** (112 BPM, a fiddle reel over guitar and bodhrán, tin whistle, pipes), **Fantasy Tavern** (110 BPM, a jig on dulcimer, lute, hand drum, accordion and recorder), **Latin** (130 BPM, bossa nova: nylon guitar, upright bass, bongos, agogô, flute), **Plaza** (104 BPM, son clave on Latin percussion, a nylon guitar montuno, upright bass, trumpet), **Mbira Morning** (96 BPM, interlocking mbira over a didgeridoo drone, folk harp, balafon, hand chimes) and **Eastern** (84 BPM, koto, shakuhachi, taiko and shamisen over a drone).
  - Each starter is 16 bars with its sections marked and the loop around the whole song, so Play goes from the start to the end, then round again (drag the loop's In and Out on the ruler to repeat just one part). Its held strings, pads and voices swell with each phrase. Its tracks are mixed and the master set for the style, and its level is set on the master's **Level**: about -15 LUFS, and about -19 for the gentle Ambient, so switching between starters doesn't jump in loudness. Change anything; it's an ordinary project.
  - **Game music from a starter**: each section between markers is a state in the **Game** tab, so any starter (or your own song with markers) can be wired into a game: set the transitions between states, put tracks in stem groups for **Intensity**, add stingers, then export a game kit. See Game music.
- **Open** (`Ctrl+O`): load a `.fledgetone.json` project file. Like New, it replaces what's on screen straight away and clears undo (the Restore banner offers the replaced song back). If the file can't be read, a red message explains why and nothing is replaced. You can also drop a `.fledgetone.json` file anywhere on the page: a drop onto an empty timeline opens it at once, and when there's work on the timeline a dialog asks before replacing it.
- **Share**: three ways to send the song to someone:
  - **A link to this song**: the whole song travels inside the link (nothing is uploaded anywhere), and opening it opens the song in Fledgetone, ready to play and change. **Copy link**, or **Share…** for your device's share menu. Sound files (audio clips, and Sampler, Slicer, Drum Rack and Multi Sampler sounds) can't go in a link, so the dialog says which tracks would be silent; frozen tracks just play live. Links are packed tight (less than half the length they used to be), but a very long one can be cut off by some chat apps: the dialog warns when it's long. Links made before still open.
  - **The song as an MP3**: the whole song mixed, handed to your share menu where the browser can (phones, Windows, Safari on a Mac), or downloaded.
  - **The project file**: everything, sound files included, shared or downloaded the same way.

  Opening a shared link on a page that already has work on its timeline asks first.
- **Save** (`Ctrl+S`): the first time, choose the folder and the file name; after that, Save writes straight back to that same file, as a desktop DAW does (the Save button's tooltip names it). A project you opened with **Open** saves back to the file it came from.
- **Save As…** (`Ctrl+Shift+S`, in the **File** menu): save to another folder or under another name (a copy, a version); from then on, Save writes there. The picker opens in the folder you last saved a project to.
- Choosing where to save needs Chrome or Edge on a computer. Other browsers (Safari, Firefox, phones) save the file to their downloads folder instead.
- **New**, a shared link or **Restore** starts a song that isn't that file: its first Save asks where again, so nothing overwrites the file by mistake.
- Keep your project files: they're the only way to move a project to another browser or computer.

A project file holds the tracks, clips, mixer and effect settings, tempo and its changes, time signatures, swing, loop, markers, the whole Master tab (Glue, OTT, Stereo Width, Mono below 120 Hz, Level, Loud master, Clipper, master reverb and delay), the game setup (states, transitions, Intensity, stingers, game sounds), frame rate, and every sound it uses (audio clips, Slicer and Sampler sounds). It doesn't hold the listening volume, zoom, snap or ruler setting. Your saved sounds and stars aren't in project files: keep them with **File › Save my sounds to a file…** (see Favourites and My sounds).

Whether it comes from **New**, **Open** or **Restore**, a project opens showing the start of the timeline, with the playhead at its loop's In point; if that's off the first screen, the view scrolls to it instead. It never keeps the previous project's scroll position.

### Autosave

The app also saves your work in this browser half a second after every change. The project bar shows **Saved in this browser · <time>**. Next time you open the app (or reload the page), your last song opens again, as you left it. Pocket does the same.

- When **New**, **Open** or a shared link replaces a song, a banner offers the replaced song back: **Restore** brings it back (and offers the one on screen in its place), **Dismiss** forgets it. The offer stays across reloads until you choose; it says how many clips the song has (or markers, when it has no clips yet) and when it was saved. Only the most recently replaced song is kept.
- If autosave fails, the project bar says why in red: the project is too big for browser storage (about 5 MB per site, usually because of big Sampler sounds), storage is full (Dismiss the old session to make room), or the browser blocks storage for this site. While it's failing, closing or reloading the tab asks first, so you can **Save**.
- If your last session can't be read (it may be damaged, or from a newer version of Fledgetone), a banner says so and it's kept apart in this browser, so nothing overwrites it. If there's no room to keep it apart, the app stops autosaving rather than write over it, and the project bar says so: use **Save**.
- Autosave is per browser and per site, and private windows don't keep it. Use **Save** for anything you care about.

### Versions: go back to earlier

**File › Versions…** lists earlier versions of your songs that this browser keeps, the song open first, each with when it was kept ("10 min ago", "yesterday 14:02"), how many tracks and bars it has, and why it was kept when it wasn't just while you worked. Click one to go back to it: after you confirm, it opens as the song, and the song you had open is kept as a version first, so going back can itself be undone from the same list. Under the list: *Your songs are files. If this app disappeared tomorrow, they would still open at fledgetone.com.*

- While you work, a version is kept every couple of minutes (with autosave), and one just before **New**, **Open**, a shared link or going back to a version replaces the song. A song that hasn't changed isn't kept twice.
- Old versions thin out: every one from the last hour, then one per 10 minutes for the last day, then one a day for 30 days. Each song's newest version stays however old it is (a song you replaced may be nowhere else), and at most 300 versions are kept in all.
- Versions keep the song, not its sound files: those stay in the browser's sound library, and **Free unused** leaves every sound a kept version uses.
- Versions are kept per browser, like autosave. Where the browser blocks storage (some private windows), the list says so, and autosave and **Save** work as before.

## Playing a MIDI keyboard

Click the **MIDI** button (the keyboard icon) in the toolbar, then **Connect MIDI devices**. Chrome and Edge connect at once; Firefox asks for permission first; Safari has no MIDI support (use the computer keyboard piano instead). The app reconnects by itself next time.

- **Input** picks one device or **All inputs**. The light and readout show the last thing played.
- Your playing goes to the **armed** track if one is armed, otherwise the **selected** track, through its instrument, mixer and effects, so you hear what you'll record.
- Velocity, note-off, the sustain pedal and all-notes-off work on every instrument. The pitch bend wheel (±2 semitones) works on the recorded instruments, Slap Bass, Sub Bass, 808, Lead, Space Clav, Brass Stabs, Pad and plugin instruments. When you record, the bend and mod wheels go into the clip's **Pitch bend** and **Mod wheel (dynamics)** lanes (see Expression lanes), and the sustain pedal into how long the notes ring.
- On a drum kit, the standard General MIDI drum pads (kick 36, snare 38, hats 42 and 46) play the kit's sounds.
- **Knobs and faders** move the settings you map them to (see MIDI learn, under Automation). Knobs that aren't mapped go to plugin instruments as MIDI, and when recording go into the clip's controller lanes.
- **Note repeat** (in the MIDI popover), for finger drumming: pick a rate (1/8, 1/16, 1/32, or their triplets) and a pad held down on a drum kit, the Drum Rack or the Slicer plays again at that rate for as long as you hold it. While the song plays the repeats land on the song's grid (struck just before a grid line, the first repeat waits for the next one, so it doesn't flam); stopped, they keep the tempo. A pad that sends pressure (aftertouch) sets the repeats' loudness as you press harder or lighter; otherwise they play as hard as you struck. It works on the computer keyboard piano too (at its fixed velocity), and recording keeps every repeat as a hit of its own, on its grid line. **Off** turns it off; the choice is kept in this browser.
- **MPE keyboards** (each note on its own channel, with its own bend and pressure): tick **MPE keyboard** in the MIDI popover. A note's own bend (±48 semitones, the MPE standard) and pressure then move only that note, and recording puts them into the note (its Note bend and Note pressure lanes). The Multi Sampler plays them per note; other instruments bend as a whole.

### The computer keyboard as a piano

In the MIDI popover, tick **Computer keyboard piano**. `A` `S` `D` `F` `G` `H` `J` `K` `L` `;` `'` are the white keys from C, `W` `E` `T` `Y` `U` `O` `P` the black keys, and `Z` / `X` move an octave down or up. Notes play at a fixed velocity. While it's on, those keys play notes instead of their usual jobs (`L`, `O`); Space and the other shortcuts still work. It's off by default.

**Tools › Piano** (or search *piano*) opens the **on-screen piano**: the same keys drawn as a keyboard, each with its letter, lit as you play them (from the computer keyboard, a MIDI keyboard or a click). Opening it turns the computer keyboard piano on; closing it turns it off again (unless it was on before). Its **Z** and **X** buttons change the octave. Drag it by its top row to move it out of the way.

### Typing notes in (note entry)

Placing notes one by one with the mouse is slow. Instead, open a clip in the piano roll and click **Type notes** at the top of it (or tick **Note entry** on the on-screen piano). A cyan cursor appears in the grid, after the clip's last note:

- **Each key you play goes in at the cursor**, and the cursor moves on by the **New note** length (or the **Grid**, if that's longer). You don't have to play in time: one key after another writes a melody.
- **Keys held down together make a chord**: they all land at the same step, and the cursor moves on once you let go of them all.
- **→** leaves a gap (a rest) and **←** steps back, while no notes are selected.
- **Backspace** takes the last step out again (and puts the cursor back there); **Ctrl+Z** undoes as usual.
- Past the clip's end, the clip grows to fit.

It works with the computer keyboard, a MIDI keyboard and clicks on the on-screen piano, on drum tracks too (each key is a drum, as when playing them live). The notes sound on the clip's track as you play them. To change the length of the next notes, change **New note** in the piano roll; for a different loudness, play softer or harder on a MIDI keyboard, or change the notes' **Vel** afterwards.

## Recording

### Recording from a microphone

Click **Mic** in the toolbar (or **Tools › Record from the microphone**) to record your voice, a guitar, or anything on your computer's audio input. The browser asks for permission the first time. Playback starts from the playhead as you click it, so you can sing or play along; the button turns into **Stop** with a level meter. Click **Stop** and the take goes onto the selected Audio track (or a new one) exactly where you played it: the delays of the speakers and the mic are taken off, so it lines up with the beat. The take is saved in the sound library like a dropped sound file, so it can be trimmed, faded, chopped (Chop sample), frozen and exported.

The input isn't played through the speakers while you record (no feedback); use headphones to hear the music. Microphone access needs a secure page (the published site, on https).

**Lining up.** The browser's own figure for those delays is often a little off (on phones, by tens of milliseconds). To measure it instead, open the Record button's settings (the chevron beside it) and click **Line up** under **Line up mic recording**: Fledgetone plays a few soft clicks through the speakers and records them, about 3 seconds; keep quiet, and use the speakers rather than headphones. From then on, mic takes on this computer and microphone are moved back by the measured delay. **Again** measures it once more, **Forget** goes back to the browser's figure. Bluetooth headphones add a lot of delay (often 150 ms or more) that changes from moment to moment: when the output looks like Bluetooth, a note says so as a take starts; record with wired headphones.

To **resample** (record a part, then mangle it), export the part as a WAV and drop it back onto a lane, or right-click an audio clip for **Chop sample…**.

### Recording notes

To record what you play on a MIDI keyboard (or the computer keyboard piano) as notes:

1. Arm a track with **R** on its header (one track at a time; Audio tracks can't be armed).
2. Switch **Record** on (the red circle in the toolbar).
3. Press **Play**. With the **1-bar count-in** on (the default) you hear one bar of clicks first (four in 4/4), then the music and the take start together.
4. Play. Notes are placed where you heard them against the music, with their velocity and length.
5. **Pause**, **Stop** or switching Record off ends the take. It becomes a clip named **Take 1**, **Take 2** and so on, on whole bars. Switching Record on while playing starts a take right there.

- **Undo** removes the whole take in one step. With **Quantize** on, the notes are snapped when you stop, as a second step: the first `Ctrl+Z` puts them back where you played them, the second removes the take.
- **Loop recording**: with the loop on, every pass adds to the same take, so you can build a part up pass by pass. Or tick **Loop passes are takes** to keep each pass as a take of its own and pick the best afterwards (see Takes and comping).
- Takes are ordinary clips: swing, editing, Insert/Delete bars and export work on them like any other.
- **Recording settings** (the arrow next to Record): **1-bar count-in**, **Quantize** (Off, 1/16, 1/8 or 1/16T), **Metronome while playing**, **Loop passes are takes**, **Punch in / out** and **Click level**. These are kept in your browser, not in the project.

### Punch in and out

Punch replaces just one part: play along from earlier to get the feel, and only what you play between the punch **In** and **Out** is kept.

- Tick **Punch in / out** in the recording settings and type its **In** and **Out** bars, or press **= loop** to punch over the loop. Or right-click the ruler: **Punch in here** and **Punch out here** (each switches punch on).
- The punch range shows as a red bar along the bottom of the ruler.
- A MIDI take keeps the notes that start inside the range, cut off at the Out, and its clip is exactly the range. A mic recording is cut to the range too.
- With **Loop passes are takes** on, punching over the same range again adds a take each time, so you can try a fill as often as you like.

### Takes and comping

With **Loop passes are takes** ticked, recording round the loop keeps each pass as a separate take in one clip (a **take folder**), and recording over a clip of exactly the same span (the same punch range, or the loop again) adds takes to it. This works for MIDI and for the microphone. The newest take plays at first; the clip shows **NT** (N takes) by its name.

Click **NT**, or right-click the clip and choose **Takes (N)…**, to **comp**: build the part from the best bits of each take.

- Each take is a row (notes or waveform). The lit stretch of each row is what plays; the rest is shaded.
- **Drag along a take** to use it there; it snaps to beats (Shift for 16ths). A click uses that beat.
- Click a take's **name** to use it all the way through. The bin deletes a take.
- **Flatten** keeps what the comp plays as an ordinary clip and drops the other takes. (A mic comp made of several takes stays as the take it starts with when flattened: to keep a mixed comp, leave it as a folder.)
- Where a mic comp changes take, the two blend over 10 ms, so the joins don't click.
- Each change is one Undo step. Take folders save with the project, split and trim like any clip, and play the comp live and in every export. Editing a MIDI folder's notes in the piano roll works as usual, but the next comp change rebuilds the notes from the takes.

### Metronome

The **metronome** button in the toolbar clicks on every beat while playing, louder on beat 1 of each bar. It follows the tempo, ignores swing, and is never in an exported WAV. It clicks in time with the music as you hear it, allowing for the few milliseconds the master section and any compressors hold the music back. The count-in clicks even when the metronome is off.

## Exporting audio

Click **Export**. The dialog has four choices, and remembers them in this browser:

- **Range**: **Whole song** (bar 1 to the end of the last clip that plays), **Loop region** (In to Out, whether or not the loop is on), or **Intro + loop** (for games, below). For the loop, the dialog shows where it starts as a timecode at your frame rate.
- **Export**: **Mixdown** (one stereo file of everything that plays), **Stems** (called **Layers** with Intro + loop, below) or **Video (MP4)** (below). For a mixdown, **Match a loudness target** turns the whole file up or down to where it's going: **Streaming** (-14 LUFS: Spotify, YouTube, Apple Music, SoundCloud), **Loud: EDM and future bass** (-9 LUFS; streaming services turn it down to -14), **Club: bass music and hardcore** (-6 LUFS, for DJs and club systems), **Games** (-16 LUFS, room for sound effects and voices), **Podcast and video voice-over** (-16 LUFS) or **TV and broadcast** (-23 LUFS, the European standard). If a quiet mix would need its peaks pushed past -1 dBTP to get there, a look-ahead limiter holds them down (up to 8 dB of it: more only distorts, since a limiter can't take a sub-heavy drop past about -10 LUFS without bending every cycle of the bass); beyond that the file stops short and the message says by how much. With the master's **Loud master** on, the target starts on its own and the Loud master gets there instead: it pushes the mix into its clipper and limiter, measuring until it's on target (and keeps that push, so playback matches the file); the message says how hard it pushed. A game's intro and loop are only turned up or down together, never limited, so the loop's seam stays seamless. When a mixdown finishes, its loudness and peak show at the bottom of the screen. A mixdown can come with other versions too, all in one .zip: **Underscore version too** leaves out the lead melody (and any voices), so the music sits under someone talking in a video or podcast; **No-drums version too** leaves out the drums, for a calmer moment. The lead is the track named "Lead" if there is one, else the loudest part playing one line at a time (an arpeggio, a pad, chords or stabs never count, so a song without a tune has no underscore); the dialog names what each version leaves out. **Stems** (one WAV per track that plays and has clips in the range, in one .zip). Stems all start at the range start and have the same length, so they line up in another program, and each includes the track's effects, sends and the shared reverb and echo. **Bypass master compressor & limiter** (on by default) makes the stems add up to the mix as it is before the master compressor and limiter.
- **Ending**:
  - **Ring out**: the range, then the release, reverb and echo tails ring out: the file ends once everything has died away (under -70 dBFS, at the file's level after any loudness match), after a second at least and at most as long as the longest tail can ring: the reverb, a track's own Delay or Reverb, or the master delay's echoes (the dialog says how long that can be).
  - **Exact length**: exactly the range, to the sample, with a 5 ms fade at the very end so it doesn't click.
  - **Seamless loop** (Loop region only): exactly one loop long, with the tails of the pass before already ringing at its start, so it repeats in a game engine or looping player without a gap or click. It stays exactly on the beat at any tempo, fractional ones included. As a WAV (the mixdown and every stem) it also carries **loop points** (a standard `smpl` chunk covering the whole file), which Godot, GameMaker, samplers and many Web Audio loaders read, so you don't have to set the loop by hand.
- **Format**: the **file type**: **WAV** (uncompressed and exact, the only one that holds markers and loop points), **MP3** (320 kb/s, plays everywhere) or **OGG** (Ogg Vorbis, about 256 kb/s, the best compressed format for games: Godot and Unity loop it without a gap). Then **44.1 kHz** or **48 kHz** (the recorded instruments are prepared again at the rate you choose, so either is as clean as the other, whatever rate your sound card runs at); a WAV can also be **88.2** or **96 kHz**, for mastering or video work at high rates (a bigger file and a slower export; an MP3 or OGG picked after is made at 44.1 or 48). For WAV, **16-bit**, **24-bit** (the default) or **32-bit float**. A 16-bit file is dithered (a faint, steady hiss far below the music, instead of the grainy distortion rounding to 16 bits otherwise leaves on fades and reverb tails); digital silence stays silent. Always stereo. An MP3 can have a few milliseconds of silence added at its start by the format itself, so use OGG or WAV for anything that has to loop seamlessly. Compressing a file nudges its peaks up (by about half a dB on a loud, dense master), so MP3, OGG and a video's sound keep their true peaks under -2 dBTP instead of -1, and still play back under -1.

A mixdown renders on several of your computer's processor cores at once where the browser allows it (Chrome and Edge do), so a big song exports up to two or three times faster. It sounds exactly the same: tracks whose sound only meets at the master are rendered side by side and added up before the master section. Tracks that play through a group bus or return with a Compressor, Distortion, Lo-fi, multiband compressor or a plugin on it are rendered together, and so are tracks with plugins. Stems render several at a time the same way, each on a core of its own. Exporting the same song again on the same computer gives the same file, sample for sample, whatever plays in it: drums, risers and noisy synths vary from hit to hit, but the same way every time.

The footer shows the file name and its exact length. Exports include the whole mix: volumes, pans, mutes and solos, every effect, sends, the master reverb and delay, the master OTT, Stereo Width, Mono below 120 Hz, Level, Loud master and Clipper, and swing. They never include the listening volume or the level matching: what you hear is only turned up or down. The first beat is as loud as the rest, so a kick right at the start keeps its attack. If the mix would clip, the file is turned down to just under full scale (stems all by the same amount, so their levels still match).

**Markers** in the range go into the file as cue points with their names, which DAWs, audio editors and game audio tools show as markers. Their positions count from the start of the file (the range start, or IN for a loop), in the mixdown and in every stem. A **Ring out** file also includes a marker sitting exactly at the end of the range. When there are any, the **Format** section says how many go in (or that the one marker does). The loop's IN and OUT aren't written as markers.

Exporting renders faster than real time and shows its progress. It pauses playback. **Cancel** stops it and nothing is saved (the dialog stays open to try again); Esc or the X stops it and closes the dialog.

> Exports need the sounds of every recorded instrument that's playing. If one can't download (for example, when you're offline), the export stops with an error. Mute that track, or switch it to a synth, to export without it.

### Music for games: intro + loop, and layers

Game music usually plays an intro once and then loops the rest for as long as the player stays. Set the loop region to the part that should repeat (everything before its **In** is the intro) and choose **Range: Intro + loop**. The export is a .zip with:

- `<name> intro.<ext>`: bar 1 up to the loop's In, exactly, ending without a fade so it runs straight into the loop.
- `<name> loop.<ext>`: the loop region as a seamless loop (see **Seamless loop** above): exactly as long as the loop, and it repeats without a gap or click.
- For WAV, also `<name> intro+loop (loop points).wav` (untick **Also one file with loop points** to leave it out): both in one file, with the loop marked inside it. Godot (set the import's loop mode to detect from the WAV) and most samplers play the intro once and loop the rest on their own.

In your game, play the intro, then start the loop the moment the intro ends, set to repeat. Intro and loop share one level and one reverb, so the change can't be heard. **Match a loudness target** measures the loop and moves both together.

Choose **Export: Layers** instead of Mixdown for **adaptive music**: an intro and a loop for every track that plays, all exactly the same lengths, so the game can play them together and fade layers in and out (drums when a fight starts, strings when the player finds something). They share one level, so turned all the way up they make the mix.

### Channel kit

**File › Channel kit (streams, videos)…** (or search *channel kit*) makes music for the start, the breaks and the end of your streams and videos from one song, as one .zip, so everything sounds like it belongs together. The dialog lists each file and the bars it comes from:

- **Intro** (about 6 seconds of the main part, fading out) and **Outro** (the song's ending, about 10 seconds, from a phrase line).
- Three seamless loops for a stream's screens: **Starting soon** (the main part: the busiest, most important section, up to 32 bars), **Be right back** (the main part without its lead melody, or without its drums) and **Ending** (a calmer section of at least 4 bars, or the main part without drums). In OBS, add one as a Media Source with **Loop** on: it repeats with no gap.
- Three short **alerts** for a follow, a subscribe and a big moment, in the song's key and tempo: your song's own upbeat stingers first (Victory, Level up, Item found, Checkpoint), made ones for the rest.
- A **transition whoosh**, for a stinger transition between scenes.
- A README saying what each file is for.

Every file is a WAV at 48 kHz, 24-bit (what streaming software and video run at). **Loudness** sets the level: the music is turned up or down by one amount for every piece (worked out on Starting soon), so a calmer piece stays calmer and you set the volume once; nothing is limited, so the loops stay seamless.

### Beat store package

**File › Beat store package…** makes everything you need to sell a beat, as one .zip, every file named with the title, tempo and key (for example "Night Drive - 140 BPM - A minor"):

- **Tagged MP3 preview**: the beat with your producer tag laid over it, for the store page and socials, so nobody can use it without buying it. **Tag sound** is the tag you recorded in **Arrange a beat › Producer tag**, or **Load** another short recording of your tag (it's kept in this browser for next time, never in a project); play it **At the start** and/or **Then every** so many seconds, at the **Tag level** you choose. The beat dips 4 dB under each tag so it's heard. Without a tag sound the preview is the plain beat.
- **Untagged WAV**: 24-bit, 44.1 kHz, the full-quality beat for buyers.
- **Stems (track-outs)**: one 24-bit WAV per track, all starting at bar 1, before the master compressor and limiter, so they add up to the mix in any studio.
- **Cover image** (optional, JPEG, PNG or WebP), and a short info file with the tempo, key and loudness.

The **Key** comes from the notes (you can pick another, or leave it out of the names). **Loudness** keeps the mix's level or matches a loudness target, as in the Export dialog.

### Video (MP4)

Choose **Export: Video (MP4)** to turn the song (or the loop region) into a video for YouTube, TikTok, socials or a game's trailer, with the mix as its sound. Pick its **Frame rate**: **Smooth 60 fps** (the notes and playhead glide; the default) or **30 fps** (a smaller file, made faster). It's made at a high quality, so it still looks sharp after TikTok or YouTube compress it again. Under **Title and marks**, type the video's **Title** (blank: the project's name) and the line **Under it** (blank: the tempo), or untick either to leave it out, and choose whether to show the **Section names** (from your markers), the **Time** and the **Waveform**. Whatever you leave out gives its room to the picture. A small *made with fledgetone.com* mark always stays at the bottom (except on your own video: see below). Pick its **Shape**: **Widescreen 16:9** (1920×1080, for YouTube, a trailer or a website) or **Portrait 9:16** (1080×1920, for TikTok, YouTube Shorts and Instagram Reels). By default a video shows the title and the line under it, the whole song's waveform with a playhead, the section you're in (from your markers) and the time. It's usually made faster than real time (30 fps is quicker than 60).

**Picture** chooses what fills the middle:

- **Showcase: the notes playing** (File › **Showcase video…** opens Export set to it, or search *showcase*): every note of the song on a piano roll that scrolls past a red playhead. Each note lights up as the playhead reaches it, glows while it's held and dims once it's played, in its track's colour. Only what you hear is shown: muted tracks (and tracks a solo leaves out) are left out, and notes hidden by a trimmed clip edge aren't drawn. Portrait shows less of the song at once (about 4½ seconds across, widescreen about 8), so the notes stay big on a phone. Two more choices under **Picture**:
  - **Tracks**: **One roll** puts every track's notes on one piano roll, the drums in a band of their own underneath and the track names above it; when two tracks play the same note at once (a doubled part), each gets half of its row, so they don't merge into one. **A lane per track** gives each track a band of its own with its name at the left, fitted to that track's notes, so a big arrangement stays readable. **Auto** (the default) uses lanes from 7 tracks.
  - **Hits**: what a note does as the playhead reaches it: **Sparkles** (a small star and a few dots), **Rings**, or **Just the glow**. The busier the song, the smaller the glow and the hits, so they don't cover the notes.
- **Spectrum** in any of the Mixer's spectrum styles (LED bars, Neon city, Grid terrain, Radar HUD, Data rain, Holo pigeons), moving with the music.

**Match a loudness target** turns it up or down to a target (YouTube plays at -14 LUFS). With a reference video loaded, **Put the music on** *your video* uses your video as the picture instead (see Scoring to a video); in Portrait it's fitted into the tall frame. Then the picture is only your video, with no title or marks. Tick **Keep the video's own sound too** to mix its sound effects or dialogue under the music.

Videos need Chrome or Edge (or another browser with WebCodecs) on a site served over https. On Windows and Mac they're H.264 with AAC sound, which plays everywhere; where the browser doesn't have those, they're VP9 or AV1 with Opus sound, which browsers, VLC, YouTube and Discord play but some older players don't.

## Game music

Fledgetone makes music for games that reacts to play: a song's sections become **states** the game switches between, its tracks **layers** it fades in and out, with **stingers** for moments, and exports that carry it all into a game engine. Any song with markers works: open a starter (File › New…) or your own song, and its sections are the states.

### Game preview: hear it the way the game will play it

The **Game** tab of the bottom panel (the **Game** button at the top right opens it) is for rehearsing adaptive music, the kind that changes with what the player is doing:

- **States**: every section between your markers is a button (Intro, Explore, Combat, Boss...). Click one and the song moves there **on the next bar** (or next beat, or at once: the menu above them) and loops it, the way a game switches music. The playing state is green; a waiting one pulses amber.
- **Transitions** (click to open, under the states): a matrix of how each state hands over to each other one. Rows are the state you come **from** (and **Any**, for a rule that applies from every state unless that row has its own); columns are the state you go **to**. Click a cell and pick:
  - **Cut** (straight in), **Fill** (a snare fill over the beat before, on the song's own kit), **Riser** (over the bar before; the switch waits a bar if there isn't a whole one left), **Impact** (a hit on the new state's downbeat), **Crossfade** (the old state fades out as the new one fades in, over 1 to 16 beats), or **Stinger** (one of your stingers plays on the line, and the new state comes in after it: a 2-bar stinger bridges 2 bars), or **Bridge** (a bar or two written to lead into the new state, then the state).
  - **Bridge** writes the lead-in from your song: it looks at what the new state opens on and plays the chord that pulls hardest into it (its dominant seventh: G7 into C), with the bass stepping up into the new root and the drums filling the last beat. **2 bars** turns through the chord before that first (ii before a major chord, iv before a minor one). It plays on your song's own chord part, bass and drums, through its mix, in the new state's tempo and time signature. Change the song and the bridge follows.
  - **When**: on the next bar (the default), on the next beat, on the next **phrase** (every 2, 4 or 8 bars counted from the state's start, so the music turns where a phrase does), at the **end of the loop** (when the state comes round, or its shuffled piece ends), or at once.
  - A switch with no rule cuts on the next bar. Rules are saved with the project.
  - Switching states on the timeline plays the cuts, fills, risers, impacts, stinger bridges and written bridges (on preview instruments), and waits for the phrase or the loop's end; a crossfade needs two parts of the song at once, so there it cuts. **Audition like the game** (below) plays every rule exactly.
- **Intensity**: the music a game turns up as things get exciting. Give each track a **stem group** with the list beside it: **Ambient**, **Percussion**, **Tension**, **Climax** or **Other…** (any name); **Auto-assign** puts drums in Percussion, effects in Tension, leads in Climax and everything else in Ambient. Each group in use gets a **curve**: how loud its tracks play (up) at each intensity (across). Drag a point to move it, click empty space to add one, double-click a point to remove it; the dashed line is the intensity now. Then scrub **Intensity** (here, or next to Swing in the header) while the song plays: the groups crossfade along their curves, smoothly, the way the game's audio code will mix them. Tracks without a group always play at full level, and the on/off switches still mute a track outright.
  - The mixdown and video exports play at the Intensity you set; stems, layers and the game kit stay at full level, so the game can apply the curves itself (the curves go into the kit's files).
  - Stem groups, the curves and the intensity are saved with the project. Changing a track's group is an Undo step; moving the slider and the curves isn't.
- When it sounds right, export the sections with **Intro + loop** and the layers with **Layers** (below) for your game engine.

**Stingers** (at the bottom of the panel) are short cues a game plays over its music when something happens. Choose one from **+ Add a stinger…**: **Victory** (a fanfare), **Level up** (a rising arpeggio), **Item found**, **Checkpoint**, **Danger** (tense low pulses) or **Game over** (falling to a low minor chord). Each is written in the song's key, worked out from its notes and shown next to the heading, and plays at the song's tempo.

- Click a stinger to fire it over the music, the way the game will: on the next bar, the next beat or at once (the same choice as the states). It lights up when it plays.
- Pick its **instrument** from the list beside it. The dice makes **another version** (a different rhythm or register), written again in the song's current key, so after a key change or new chords it follows.
- The list button **puts it on the timeline** at the playhead, on a new track, as an ordinary clip you can edit in the piano roll.
- **Export stingers (.zip)** renders each one as a WAV through the song's master section, ringing out, ready for the game.
- Stingers are saved with the project. Adding, changing or deleting one isn't an Undo step (putting one on the timeline is).

**Loop variations** (under the stingers) export 2, 3 or 4 **takes** of the loop region, so a player who stays in one place doesn't hear the very same loop over and over: the game picks a different take each time round. **A** is the loop as written; **B** ends with a snare fill into the loop point, **C** with a tom fill, and **D** drops the hats for the first half with a kick pickup at the end (the fills go on the first drum track that plays through the loop's last beat). With **Human feel** ticked, tracks without the Human insert get a light one, so every take's timing and dynamics differ too. **Export** saves a .zip of seamless WAV loops (with loop points), all exactly the loop's length and at one level, so the game can switch takes at the loop point without a jump.

### Audition like the game

**Audition like the game** (in the Game tab) renders the states, their stems, the stingers and the transition sounds exactly as the game kit exports them (it takes a few seconds, more for a long song), then plays them with the kit's own web player. Big pads switch states (a waiting one pulses until its line), stinger pads fire stingers on the next beat, and **Intensity** crossfades the stem groups. Every switch follows your transition matrix, crossfades included, so what you hear is what the game will do. The song stops while it's open.

### Costumes: the same music played another way

**Costumes** (in the Game tab) are the song played another way: each track on another instrument, or silent, and maybe faster or slower. The game swaps costumes mid-loop and the tune carries on in its new clothes: the 8-bit version in a retro room, a music box in a dream, the night version after dark, a hurry-up when time runs low.

- Pick a preset and **Add costume**: **8-bit** (every part on the chip sounds), **Music box** (the tune and chords on a music box, no drums), **Solo piano**, **Night** (no drums, brass on soft strings, 10% slower), **Hurry up** (20% faster, same pitch) or **Your own** (nothing changed yet).
- Open a costume to rename it (its name is what the game asks for), set its tempo, or pick each track's instrument: as it is, silent, or any other (drum parts can only go to another kit). Notes are moved into the new instrument's range.
- Hear them in **Audition like the game** (the **Costume** buttons). The game kit renders every state in every costume, lined up so a player can swap at any moment.

### How each state plays: shuffled pieces and silences

Under the states, **How each state plays** sets, for each state:

- **Order**: as written (a loop), or **shuffle 2-, 4- or 8-bar pieces**: the game plays the state's pieces in a random order, never the same piece twice running, so a long stay never sounds quite the same.
- **Silence**: none, or a silence between plays (4 to 12 s, 15 to 45 s, 1 to 3 min), a different length each time: music that comes and goes, so it still means something when it comes back.

The timeline plays a state as written; the game kit's players (and Audition like the game) do the shuffling and the silences.

### Effects in the game

The game kit's players can shape the music for what's going on, with no extra files: **underwater** (a low-pass and a big room), **muffled** (behind a wall, the pause menu), **low health** (duller, further away), **dream** (soft, washed in reverb) and **none**, gliding over a second or so. Try them in Audition like the game (the **Effect** buttons). In code it's `setEffect("underwater")` (web), `set_effect("underwater")` (Godot) or `SetEffect("underwater")` (Unity); `setFilter` and `setReverb` set the low-pass and the reverb directly. Sound effects aren't touched.

### Parameters: one number from the game, several changes in the music

A parameter is a number the game sets, 0 to 1, that moves several things in the music at once: **danger** raising the intensity as enemies close in, **health** muffling the music as it runs out, **depth** under water. The game doesn't need to know how the music is mixed; it just calls `setParameter("danger", 0.7)` (web), `set_parameter("danger", 0.7)` (Godot) or `SetParameter("danger", 0.7f)` (Unity). It's how games drive their music in FMOD and Wwise.

In the Game tab, **Parameters** adds one from a preset (or **your own**, empty):

- **danger**: the intensity rises from 15% to full, and the music opens up from a slightly dull 7 kHz over the first half.
- **health** (starts at 100%): below half, the music muffles (down to 1.2 kHz) and dips to 75%.
- **depth**: deeper is more muffled (down to 600 Hz) and roomier.
- **distance**: far from where the music plays (a radio, a stage), quieter, duller and roomier.
- **tension**: your stem groups come in one after another as it rises (each over its own stretch).

Each thing a parameter moves is a line you can change: **what** it moves (**Intensity**, **Filter** in Hz, **Reverb**, **Volume**, or one stem group's **Layer** level), what it **goes from** and **to**, and over which part of the parameter (**as danger goes from 0 to 50%**: before that it holds its first value, after it its last). **move something else…** adds a line. **starts at** is where the parameter is when the game starts.

When several parameters move the same thing, the strongest wins: the highest intensity and reverb, the lowest filter; volumes and a layer's levels multiply. The effects (above) and `setFilter` / `setReverb` combine the same way. A song whose parameters move the intensity follows them rather than `setIntensity`.

Move them in **Audition like the game**: each parameter gets a slider under **Intensity**. **Try it in a game** drives a parameter called danger (or threat, tension, alert) with the guard, like the intensity, and one called health (or hp, life) with your hearts. The game kit carries them all (its README lists them); the FMOD and Wwise packages leave them out.

### Try it in a game

**Game → Try it in a game…** (or **Try it in a game** in the Game tab) drops your song into a little game so you can hear it react to play. Pick the game at the top (it remembers your pick):

- **3D arena**: you're a little robot; a one-eyed cube guard patrols the far side of a fenced field with eight coins.
- **2D platformer**: run and jump to the flag at the end of a side-scrolling level, past gaps, spikes and one-eyed blobs, collecting twelve coins. **Arrows** or **A/D** run, **Space**, **W** or **Up** jumps (hold it to jump higher), and landing on a blob squashes it. A blob that spots you chases you (the chase state, with the intensity rising as it gets closer); falling into a gap or onto spikes costs a heart; reaching the flag plays the win stinger and the win state.
- **Rhythm game**: your whole song plays and its own notes fall down four lanes; hit **D F J K** (or the arrows) as each reaches the line. The notes are the song's: kicks on the left, snares on the right, and the melody moving across the lanes the way the tune moves. **Difficulty**: **Easy** keeps the beat, **Normal** adds the tune and holds (keep the key down to the end of a long note for a bonus), **Hard** adds its quick notes and pairs. Each hit is **Perfect**, **Great** or **Good** (early or late is shown), a note let past is a **Miss** and muffles the song for a moment, and the end shows your grade (S to D), accuracy and best combo. If every hit feels late (Bluetooth headphones play late), set **Timing** to move the notes to what you hear. It renders the song first, like an export, then counts you in.

In the 3D arena and the platformer:

- In the arena **Arrows** or **WASD** walk and **Space** jumps (land on the guard to beat it). In every game **R** restarts and **Esc** closes; while it's open the game takes its keys, so Space jumps instead of playing the song.
- The game starts at once. The music renders the way the game kit exports it (the first time takes a while for a long song; opening it again on an unchanged song is instant). The guard (or the blobs) nap until the music is ready, so you can walk around and grab coins meanwhile.
- Get close and the guard spots you: the music switches to the chase state through your transition matrix, and **Intensity** rises the closer the guard gets. Get away and it gives up, back to exploring. A hit costs a heart; three and it's game over (at one heart left the music goes dull and far away: the players' low-health effect). Bonking the guard (or reaching the platformer's flag) plays the win stinger and the win state.
- Coins, footsteps, jumps, hits and the bonk play your song's game sounds when it has fitting ones, else the preview's own.
- **Music wiring** shows what plays for each moment (exploring, chasing, beaten, each stinger and sound), guessed from your names; change any of them there. The panel at the top left shows the state playing and the intensity, and the list under it says what the music just did and why.

### Sound effects maker

The **Sound FX** tab of the bottom panel (or **Tools › Sound effects maker**, or search *sound effects*) makes the sounds games are full of: menus, hits, pickups, magic, footsteps, jingles and ambience loops. It has four parts: **Make a sound**, **Make a set**, **My sounds** and **In this game**.

**Make a sound.** Pick a group (**Retro**, **UI**, **Combat**, **Movement**, **Rewards**, **Magic & Sci-fi**, **Jingles**, **Foley**, **Loops**, **Nature**, **Places**, **Creatures**), then a kind of sound: **Click**, **Confirm**, **Error**, **Sword swing**, **Heavy impact**, **Footstep**, **Gem**, **Level up**, **Teleport**, **Victory**, **Rain**, **Forest**, **Thunder**, **Wolf** and about a hundred more. Each click makes a new one of that kind and plays it. **Mutate** makes one like it but a little different; **Surprise me** any kind.

- **Engine.** Every kind except **Retro** is made with the **modern** engine: clean waves (no gritty fold-back on high notes), natural decays, stereo, and two things that make a sound real. **Recordings**: a layer's **Source** can be a recording of the real thing (footsteps on stone, grass, snow, wood, carpet and dirt; wood, metal, glass and stone hits; punches, cloth, blades, latches, coins, doors, pages, cards, dice, UI clicks, splashes, bubbles, lasers, explosions, rain, water, fire, engines), each with several takes, so every variation of a sound plays a different one; they load when a sound first needs them (17 MB for all of them, mostly the 20-second nature loops; each group loads on its own). **Body**: a layer can strike something of **wood**, **metal**, **glass**, **stone**, **plastic** or **skin**, which rings at the layer's pitch the way that material does (**Ring**: how long; **Body mix**: how much you hear). Each layer can sit anywhere left to right and move across (**Pan**, **Pan to**: a sound passing by) spread its noise wide (**Width**), and swell and drop at random as wind does (**Gusts**, with **Gust speed**; **Wobble** then moves the filter with the gusts, so a whistle rises as the wind blows harder); echo bounces side to side and the reverb has the early reflections of a real room. Modern sounds save as 24-bit stereo WAV. **Retro** sounds, and any sound made before the modern engine, keep the **classic** engine (mono, 8-bit character) and sound exactly as they did; **Engine** in **Finish** switches a sound between the two. **Nature** and **Places** are real field recordings looped for 20 seconds (a different stretch each variation): **Forest** birdsong, **Night** insects, **Wind in trees**, **River**, **Ocean waves**, **Downpour**, **Thunderstorm**, **Cave** drips, a **City street**, a **Cafe** and a **Market**, plus single **Thunder** claps rolling away. **Creatures** are recorded animals, each a little higher or lower every time so a flock doesn't sound like one bird: songbirds, crows, owls, seagulls, dogs, cats (and a purring loop), wolves, frogs, horses, cows, roosters, chickens, sheep, pigs, bees and flies, and for games a **Monster roar**, a **Zombie**, a **Goblin** and a **Squeak**. **Make a set** has them as **Nature & places** and **Animals & creatures**. The **Foley** group is everyday things being handled: doors opening and closing, a page, a book, a card dealt, dice, glass breaking, a bubble, slime and a sci-fi door; **Make a set** has them as **Foley & props**. The recordings are public domain (CC0): see the Credits.
- **Layers.** A sound is up to six layers played together: a modern hit is a thump, a crack and a tail; a sword swing a whoosh and a ring. Each layer has its **Source** (square, saw, triangle, sine, 8-bit noise, soft noise, deep noise, FM, bell or crackle), its pitch (**Pitch**, **Slide** and its **Curve**, a **Jump** partway through, **Notes** to play in turn such as *0 4 7 12* for an arpeggio, **Vibrato**, **Repeat** for a warble), its loudness over time (**Attack**, **Hold**, **Punch**, **Decay**, **Tremolo**), a filter (**low-pass** or **band-pass**, **Cutoff**, **Resonance**, **Sweep**, **Wobble**, **High-pass**, **Crush**), and its **Level** and **Start** in the sound. **+ Layer** adds one; **Copy layer** copies the one you're on; untick **On** to hear the sound without it. Letting go of any slider plays the result.
- **Finish** goes over the whole sound: **Pitch** (every layer), **Drive** (soft clipping: grit and weight), **Echo** (with its time and feedback), **Reverb** and **Room**, **Loudness** and **Volume**, and **Loop**.
- **Even loudness.** Every sound is made to a loudness for its kind (**soft** for menus, **normal**, **loud** for impacts and explosions), never above -1 dBFS, with the silence after it trimmed and its ends faded so it never clicks. Sounds you make on different days still sit together in a game.
- **Loops** (the **Loops** group, or tick **Loop** on any sound) render exactly the loop's length and join up seamlessly: engines, wind (a **Breeze**, **Gusty wind**, a **Storm** and **Howling wind**), rain, fire, a stream, alarms, force fields. Saved as WAV they carry loop points, so Godot, samplers and most engines loop them on their own.
- **Tune to key** (on when the song has notes) puts pitches, jumps and the notes of jingles in the key the song is in, so a coin or a level-up sits in the music. The key and the first layer's note show next to it.
- **Variations.** A sound a game plays over and over (a footstep, a hit) sounds mechanical if it's the very same every time. Set **Variations** (up to 8) and **Spread** (how far their pitch moves) and **Hear variations**; saving then makes them all, numbered (*footstep_01.wav*, *footstep_02.wav*...), in a zip.
- **Save** as **WAV** or **OGG** (small; Godot and Unity import it), at 44.1 or 48 kHz. File names are ready for a game: lower case with underscores (*open_menu.wav*). **Add to song** puts the sound at the playhead on the selected Audio track (or a new one). **Keep** puts it in My sounds. **Add to game** puts it in the song's game sounds (below), with its variations: the game kit carries it. **Recent** brings back the last twelve you made.

**Make a set.** Every sound a kind of game needs, in one go: **Menus & UI** (12 sounds), **Retro platformer**, **Action RPG**, **Sci-fi shooter**, **Casual & puzzle**, **Ambience loops**, **Nature & places**, **Animals & creatures** and **Foley & props**. The sounds of a set share a style (the same tone, register and space), so they sound like a family, and they're levelled to sit together. Play each one, **↻** for another of it in the set's style, **Edit** to shape it in Make a sound, **✕** to leave it out, **New style** to make the whole set again differently, and **Hear all**. **Download set** saves a zip with a folder of files named for the game (with variations, if you set them) and a *sounds.json* that opens the set again in My sounds (**Import**), to change it later.

**In this game** lists the song's game sounds: what the **game kit** export carries next to the music, each named for the game (*sword_hit*) and played with `playSfx("sword_hit")` in its players. Play each one, rename it, set its **Variations**, **Edit** it (then **Update in game** puts your changes back) or remove it. **Add all to game** on a set adds the whole set. They're saved with the song.

**My sounds** keeps the sounds you **Keep** in this browser: play, **Edit**, save or delete them. **Export all** saves them to a file, to back them up or share them; **Import** opens one (or a set's *sounds.json*).

The same settings always make the same sound, so a sound you kept can be made again exactly.

### Micro-variations for game sounds

A sound a game plays again and again (a footstep, a sword swing, a menu click) sounds mechanical if it's the very same every time, so games pick one of several slightly different takes at random. Right-click a short clip (a beat or two) and choose **Generate variations…**:

- **Takes**: 4, 6 or 8. The first is the clip as it is; the others are drawn within the bounds below, spread across them so no two come out nearly the same. **Reroll** draws new ones.
- **Pitch** (± %, 5% is about a semitone either way; a higher take is a little shorter, as on a sampler, so it works on drums and recordings too), **Brightness** (± %: the track's Filter cutoff, or a gentle low-pass if the Filter is off), **Length** (± ms: how long each note is held before it lets go) and **Level** (± %).
- **Keep the reverb and echo**: off by default, since game sounds are usually dry and the game adds its own space.
- The numbered buttons play each take. **Save .zip** saves them as `<clip>_01.wav` to `_08.wav` (one shared level, the loudest at -1 dBFS, with the silence at the end trimmed); **Lay out on the timeline** puts them end to end on a new Audio track right after the clip.

### The game kit: everything for the game in one file

**Export game kit (.zip)** in the Game tab makes everything a game needs to play your music, and your sound effects, the way the panel does. Choose what it's **For** first:

- **Godot 4 project**: unzip it into your Godot project's folder (the one with `project.godot`). You get `bos_music/`, with the song's folder, the player, an **example scene** (`bos_music/<song>_example.tscn`: open it and press **F6**, then **1**, **2**... switch states, **Up** / **Down** intensity, **Space** a stinger, **A**, **B**... the sound effects) and a README with the steps to use it in your game: add `AdaptiveMusic.gd` as an autoload called **Music**, `Music.load_song("res://bos_music/<song>/")` once, then `Music.play(...)`, `Music.set_state(...)` and so on.
- **Unity project**: unzip it into your Unity project's folder (the one with `Assets`). Everything lands in `Assets/Fledgetone`: the player, the song under `Resources/BosMusic/<Song>`, an **example** component (make an empty GameObject, **Add Component › Adaptive Music Example**, press **Play**: buttons for every state, stinger and sound effect, and an intensity slider) and a README.
- **Any engine**: one folder with everything and the players for the web, Godot and Unity side by side, as below.

What's inside:

- `states/`: every state (section between markers) as a **seamless loop**, a whole number of bars long, with loop points.
- `stems/<state>/`: when tracks have stem groups, the same loop split by group (tracks without a group go in **Main**), every stem exactly the same length: played together at full level they make the mix.
- `transitions/`: the fill, riser and impact sounds your rules use (a fill or a riser starts just before the switch; an impact on it) and the bridges (`bridge into <state>.wav`: it plays on the switch and the state comes in after it), and `stingers/`: every stinger.
- `sfx/`: the song's sound effects (added with **Add to game** in the Sound FX tab), each with its variations (`sword_hit_01.wav`, `sword_hit_02.wav`...). Looping ones (an engine, the rain) carry loop points.
- `audio_manifest.json`: the tempo, the time signature, the sample rate, the key, every state (its loop, its stems, its length in bars, seconds and samples), the transition rules by state name, every stinger (with how many bars it bridges), each stem group's **intensity curve** ([intensity, level] points, both 0 to 1) as the recommended gain curve for the game's audio code, and every sound effect with its files.
- Ready-made players that read the manifest and do all of it (switching by your rules on the beat, crossfades, stinger bridges, intensity, sound effects): `adaptive-music.js` for a web game, `AdaptiveMusic.gd` for Godot 4, and `AdaptiveMusic.cs` for Unity (scheduled on Unity's audio clock, so changes land exactly on the beat). Each has `play`, `setState` / `set_state` / `SetState`, `setIntensity` / `set_intensity` / `SetIntensity`, `stinger`, `playSfx` / `play_sfx` / `PlaySfx` (a different variation each time, never the same one twice in a row; a looping sound plays until stopped), `stopSfx` / `stop_sfx` / `StopSfx`, and `stop`. Sound effects have their own volume (`setSfxVolume`, `sfxVolume`, or Godot's `sfx_bus`).
- A README that says how to drop it in, with your own state, stinger and sound names in the examples.

### FMOD and Wwise

**File › FMOD / Wwise package…** (also in the Game tab) lays your game music out for audio middleware, in one .zip:

- `Audio/`: every state as an exact loop, its stems by stem group, the stingers and the transition sounds (48 kHz, or 44.1).
- `cues.json`: the tempo, every state (bars, seconds, samples, stems, and where it sits on one timeline), the transition rules, the stingers and the intensity curves.
- `fmod/…js`: an **FMOD Studio** script. Put it in your project's Scripts folder and choose **Scripts › Fledgetone › Import**: it builds the event with a track per stem group, every state laid out with a loop region and a marker, the tempo, an **Intensity** parameter driving each group's volume along its curve, and an event per stinger.
- `wwise/…py`: a **Wwise** script (Wwise Authoring API, Python). With Wwise open, it builds a Music Switch Container with a playlist and segment per state and a track per stem group, a State Group with a state per section, an Intensity game parameter, and the stingers as sounds.
- `README.md`: how to run them, your states, transitions and intensity curves (in dB), and the steps each tool doesn't let a script do (FMOD's transition regions, Wwise's state assignments and RTPC curves).

Versions of FMOD and Wwise differ, so each script step reports whether it worked (OK or SKIP) rather than stopping the rest.

### The web runtime kit: music with no audio files

For a web game where download size matters, **Export web runtime kit (no audio files)** (in the Game tab) makes a .zip with just:

- the song's project file (`<song>.json`, usually tens of kilobytes, with any sounds your clips play inside it),
- `bos-player.js` (about 1.1 MB, about 290 KB as a web server sends it), which **synthesizes the music in the player's browser** when the game loads, with the same engine as Fledgetone: every state (as stems, if the song has stem groups), the stingers and the transitions,
- a test page (`index.html`) and a README.

In the game: `const music = await loadSong('./song.json', { onProgress })`, then `music.play(...)`, `music.setState(...)`, `music.setIntensity(...)` and `music.stinger(...)`, exactly as with the game kit's web player. Synthesizing takes a few seconds per minute of music (longer on slow devices; a 16-bar song with three stem groups took about 12 seconds in testing), so start it while the game loads and show a progress bar. Recorded instruments download their samples from the same public libraries the app uses, so they need a connection; the synths need nothing.

## Fledgetone Pocket: on a phone

On a phone, Fledgetone opens **Pocket**: the same studio underneath (the same instruments, effects and songs) on one screen made for a thumb. A song started in Pocket opens in the studio on a computer, and back. The song you were working on opens again by itself when you come back; with none, Pocket starts with **What are we making?**: tap a starter and it plays straight away (or start a **Blank song**, or open a project file). The disc button at the top left comes back here for a new song, with a way back to the one you were on. Pocket is in its early stages, and the studio on a computer is the full experience: a notice says so each time Pocket opens, until you tick **Don't show this again**.

The screen, top to bottom:

- **The song**, its name, tempo and where it's playing. Tap it for the song's settings: its name, tempo (**−**, **+**, or **Tap** it), the key the notes follow, the **metronome**, saving and opening the project file, **Versions**, saving and opening your **My sounds**, a new song, and **Use the full studio here**. At the bottom it says whether the phone (or the browser) keeps your songs and sounds even when space runs low, with **Ask to keep it** when it doesn't promise to; save the file to be safe either way. **Share** (top right): an MP3 of the whole song to the phone's share sheet, matched to the **Loudness** picked above it (**Streaming** -14 LUFS, **Loud** -9 or **Club** -6; it starts on the Loud master's target when **Loud** is on in the mixer, else Streaming), a link to the song, and the project file.
- **Versions**: in the song's settings, **Versions: go back to earlier** lists the earlier versions this phone keeps (the same list as the studio's **File › Versions…**, see *Versions: go back to earlier*): tap one to go back to it. **What are we making?** has a **Versions** button too.
- **The song as a grid.** Each row is a **section** of the song, in order, and each column a track; each cell shows what that track plays in that section. Sections are the song's markers (the ones the studio shows on its timeline), or every 4 or 8 bars in a song without any, and each has a letter: sections that play the same thing share one (A A B A). Scroll the grid both ways; the letters and track names stay put.
  - **Tap a cell** to work on that track in that section: the bottom of the screen becomes its instrument. **Tap a letter** to pick that section: with **Loop** on, Play loops it (while the song plays it moves there on the next bar line); with **Song** on, it is just selected.
  - The selected section's **⋯** (or holding any letter) arranges the song from there: **Play it twice** (a linked copy right after it: change either and both change, until you **Make it its own**), **Move earlier** or **later**, **Build-up into it** (a riser, a snare roll and a hit on its first beat), **Bounce it to a pad** (everything playing in it made into one sound, on the first empty pad of **Your pads**, or of a new **Bounces** track: play it like a drum, see below) and **Take it out** (its bars go and the rest of the song moves up). **+** under the letters adds a section: a linked copy of the selected one, after it.
  - **+** after the tracks (or **+ Track**) adds an instrument, **Fledge Synth** (the app's own synth, starting on its Wobble bass), **Vocals** to sing on, **Your pads** (a Drum Rack: 16 pads for your own sounds), or the **Drop pad**. Your **My sounds** (saved in the studio) and starred **Favourites** are listed first; **★** an instrument to star it, here or in the studio.
  - **Loop / Song** picks what Play plays: the selected section over and over, or the whole song in order.
  - **Turn the phone** on its side and the grid turns into the studio's timeline (sections left to right), with the instrument beside it.
- **The transport**: undo, **Rec** (for the selected track), **Play** (it says what it plays: "Loop A" or "Song") and redo. The screen stays on while the song plays.
- **The instrument** of the selected track, for the selected section. Its handle makes it bigger or smaller (tap it, or drag it): its pads and steps stay the same size, and a bigger drawer shows more of them, the rest scrolling.
  - **Drums, Play**: the kit's pads, laid out like an MPC with the kick, snare, clap and hat along the bottom (and a light buzz on Android). With **Rec** on, pads you play while the section plays are written into it, on the nearest 16th.
  - **Drums, Draw**: one drum at a time (pick it along the top) and its 16ths, a bar at a time, a row per beat: tap a step to turn it on or off. Hold a lit step to **Roll** it over a beat, two beats or a bar (it speeds up from 8ths to 64ths and gets louder into the next hit, taking the place of that drum's steps there), or to choose how often it plays: **Always**, **75%**, **50%** or **25%** of the time, or only the **1st of every 2**, **2nd of every 2**, **1st of every 4** or **4th of every 4** times round (a section repeated or looped then changes a little each time; see the piano roll's Notes that don't play every time). Such steps are paler, dashed and marked (**50%**, **1:2**). **Drummer** writes the section's beat in one of 21 styles (pick again for another take), **Fill** puts a fill at the end of the section, **Swing** gives it a shuffle (tap for more), the two dice buttons roll the drum shown (the one named after it, such as **Hat**) or the whole beat (**Beat**: new hats and percussion, the same kick and snare) again, in the style the Drummer last wrote there or the one it sounds like (each roll one Undo step), **Clear** empties it, **Copy to all bars** puts the bar shown (every drum) in every bar of the section, and **Bar 1**, **Bar 2**… pick the bar (it follows the song while playing).
  - **Your pads** (a Drum Rack track) works like drums: **Play** has its 16 pads (pad 1 bottom left), **Draw** its steps a pad at a time, and **Rec** writes in what you play. An empty pad (**+ Pad 3**) opens **Pads**, which fills it: **Bounce A** puts the selected section as you hear it (everything playing, or **Just one track…**) on that pad, or pick a vocal take. Pads also sets the chosen pad's **Level** and **Pitch** (to play a bounce higher or lower), **Reverse**, and empties it (the bin). The studio's Drum Rack panel has the rest of each pad's settings.
  - **Keys, bass and synths, Play**: **Notes** are pads of the song's key, low notes at the bottom (**−** and **+** move them an octave); **Chords** are the key's chords. With **Rec** on, what you play is written in.
  - **Keys, bass and synths, Draw**: the **piano roll**, with rows of the song's key (and any other notes the part has) and a column per 16th. It opens the drawer full, and leaving it puts it back. Tap for a note as long as the pen (1/16 to a bar: tap the length to change it; it keeps the length you last drew or stretched, shared with the studio's piano roll, and starts at 1/4 for pads and chords), drag right as you draw to make it longer; drag a note to move it, drag its right end to stretch it; dragging to an edge scrolls the roll. **Chord** draws a chord of the key in one tap, **Erase** takes notes away. Tap a note to select it: the bar above makes it shorter or longer, a note down or up, **Dive**s it (it starts an octave up and falls to its pitch over a 16th; tap again to take it off), copies it after itself, sets how often it plays (**%**: always, 75%, 50%, 25%, 1st or 2nd of every 2, 1st or 4th of every 4) or deletes it. Under the roll is a **step lane** (see *Step lanes* in the piano roll): on a Fledge Synth track a row of steps for one of its macros, named on the left (tap the name for the next macro); on other tracks the Filter's cutoff. Drag a finger across the steps to set their heights; with **Erase** it clears them (a cleared step holds the value before). The studio's piano roll has every setting, step length and tool. Scroll with two fingers, or by dragging the note names or the bar numbers; tap a note name to hear it; the house button goes back to the key's home note. **Chords** fills the part with a progression in the key (held, pulsing, off-beat stabs or an arpeggio).
  - **Sound**: another instrument of the same kind (drums for drums, pitched for pitched), your Favourites and My sounds first; for pitched tracks, **Fledge Synth** too. **Share this sound** saves the track's sound as a small file to send, and **Open a sound file** puts one on the track (and in My sounds). On Your pads it's **Pads** (see above).
  - **Sound, on a Fledge Synth track**: shape the synth itself. Pick a **preset** (by group: Bass, Lead, Keys, Chords, Pad, FX, or step with **‹ ›**), then move its **macros**, big sliders named for what they do (on the Wobble bass: **Speed up**, **Squelch**, **Grit**; every bass has three). When the sound wobbles in time with the song, **Wobble rate** sets how fast, as a note length (1/1 to 1/32, triplets too), so it stays locked to the tempo. Every move is one step for Undo. The first time, tap **Start the sound to shape it** (phones only start sound after a tap). **Another instrument…** swaps it for one of the others; Play and Draw work as on any pitched track.
  - **Mix**: the track's level, reverb, mute and solo, and deleting it. **Check the drop** (also under **Mix all**) renders the selected section and says in a few lines whether it holds up against finished songs of the style you pick: gaps between the hits, whether the bass moves or only wobbles at one steady rate, whether the sub is the right size and in the middle, and whether it's loud enough. Tap a line for what to change. The studio's **Does it hit?** (Mix check) shows every measurement.
  - **Drop pad, Play**: six big pads that change the **whole mix** while you hold them: **Stutter**, **Filter**, **Tape stop**, **Crush**, **Gate** and **Echo** (see Drop Pad in the instruments). Hold two at once if you like. Pressing a pad while the song is stopped plays it (as **Play** would). While the song plays, a press starts on the next 16th and letting go ends on one, so it's always in time. With **Rec** on while the section plays, each hold is written into the section, from where it started to where you let go, over what that pad had there; the line under the pads shows the section's moves, and **Clear** takes them out. Add it with **+ Track › Drop pad**.
  - **Vocals, Sing**: **Record** (or **Rec vocal** below the grid) sings over the song from the phone's microphone: with the loop on it records the section once and stops by itself, else from the playhead until **Stop**. Wear headphones, or the microphone records the song too; wired ones, as Bluetooth headphones put the take late (a note says so while recording when the phone's output looks like Bluetooth). **Line up recording**, under the takes, measures once how late takes arrive on this phone (a few soft clicks through the speaker, recorded by the microphone, about 3 seconds: take headphones off and keep quiet), and takes are then moved back by that much. In the app, the first take starts with a word about why Fledgetone needs the microphone, then the phone asks; if it's refused, Pocket says how to allow it, with a button to the app's settings on Android. Pick the voice's sound (Clean lead, Aggressive lead, Ad-lib, Phone / radio, Dark and wide, Backing stack); each take can be **doubled** (two more performances either side), **tuned** (pitch correction to the key, gentle to robotic), put **on a pad** (the pad button: **Whole take on a pad** puts it on the first empty pad of Your pads, as recorded, without the vocal sound; **Chop onto pads** cuts it at its words and hits onto the pads of a new Drum Rack track, to play them in a new order) or deleted.
  - **Mix all** (above the grid): a mixer for every track, with a fader, mute (**M**) and solo (**S**) each, and the master's level, **Loud** (the Loud master, aimed at the style **Check the drop** is set to: Club for bass music and hardcore, Loud for future bass, and matched to it at once; you hear it level-matched, the MP3 is the loud one), **Crunch** (with Loud on: lets it clip on purpose, 70% of the studio's Crunch, matched again at once) and **Pump** (Duck to kick in that style: a hole in the bass for every kick and snare, the chords, pads and leads pumping on the kick; Undo takes it back).

**In the phone app** (Android for now): saving a project file, My sounds or an MP3 hands it to the phone's share sheet, to save to Files or Drive or send on; if the sheet is closed without picking anything, Pocket says it wasn't saved. **Open file** lists every file (Android often doesn't know a project file's type), and a file that isn't a Fledgetone project is refused with a reason. A project file opened from a file manager (**Open with**) or shared to Fledgetone from another app opens as the song. The phone's **Back** closes the sheet or dialog that's open, then leaves the starters screen, the mixer or the full piano roll; on Pocket's main screen it puts the app away, as Home does, without closing your song. When the app goes to the background, the song is saved at once, a vocal take being recorded is stopped and kept, and the song pauses; come back and press Play to carry on.

What Pocket doesn't show (automation, plugins other than Fledge Synth, inserts, game music, video) is kept as it is and edited in the studio; a track Pocket can't edit (a sampler or another plugin instrument) still plays, with its level, mute and solo under Mix. A MIDI keyboard plugged into an Android phone plays the selected track. **Use the full studio here** switches this phone to the studio, and it stays there next time; **Help › Pocket, the phone version** switches back. Adding `?pocket` or `?studio` to the address does the same on any device, and is remembered too.

## Installing Fledgetone

Fledgetone can be installed as an app from fledgetone.com: it opens in a window of its own, with its own icon in your Start menu, Dock or app list, and it opens even without an internet connection.

- **Chrome or Edge on a computer**: choose **Help › Install Fledgetone…**, or click the install icon at the right of the address bar.
- **Safari on a Mac**: **File › Add to Dock** (or **Share › Add to Dock**).
- **Safari on an iPhone or iPad**: tap **Share**, then **Add to Home Screen**.
- **Android**: the browser's menu (⋮) › **Install app** or **Add to Home screen**.
- Firefox on a computer doesn't install web apps; Fledgetone works in it as a web page.

In the installed app, the Help menu no longer offers it. The installed app and the web page share everything: your autosave, your sounds and the kept recordings. A one-file copy of Fledgetone (an HTML file opened from your computer) can't be installed; Help › Install Fledgetone tells you to open fledgetone.com instead.

**New versions.** Fledgetone looks for a new version when it opens and about every half hour while it's open (only while you're online, and not while a song plays or an export renders). A new version downloads quietly in the background; then a small notice in the bottom right says **Version … is ready** with a **Reload** button. Nothing changes until you click it: your work is autosaved first, the app reloads into the new version, **Restore** brings your song back, and **What's new** opens at the new version's changes. If a new version arrives while you play or export, the notice waits until you stop. Close it with **×** to carry on: the new version opens next time you start Fledgetone. Only the files that changed are downloaded.

## Working offline

After you've opened Fledgetone once from fledgetone.com (in Chrome, Edge, Safari or Firefox), it opens and works without an internet connection, installed or not:

- **The app itself** is kept in the browser and opens from there, offline or not, so it starts at once and isn't downloaded again.
- **Recorded instruments** download their recordings the first time a song uses them; then they're kept (Master tab › **Instruments offline** shows how many and how much space, and **Download all** gets every instrument now, for a trip). A recording is downloaded again only when its instrument is re-recorded in a new version.
- **Synths, drum machines, effects, exports and the sound effects maker** need nothing from the internet.
- **The built-in plugins** download the first time you use one and are then kept too.

**Keeping your work.** Browsers can clear a website's storage when the disk is nearly full. Fledgetone asks the browser to keep it (when you install the app, or once you've made a song); Master tab › **Instruments offline** shows **Storage: protected**, or **may be cleared by the browser if the disk gets full** with a **Protect** button to ask again. Browsers decide for themselves; installing Fledgetone usually lets it keep your storage. Whatever the browser says, **File › Save** your songs to files: that's the copy nothing can clear.

## Tips and limits

- Songs can be up to 400 bars long, with any number of tempo changes (40 to 240 BPM, to 0.01 BPM) and time signatures. The shortest loop is a 16th note.
- For EDM, set **Swing** to 0% (new blank projects start at 20%). Dubstep is usually 140 BPM with the snare on beat 3 (half-time), house around 124 to 128, drum & bass around 174.
- A project saved in this version may not open in an older copy of Fledgetone (such as a one-file copy saved earlier): open it at fledgetone.com instead.
- Recorded instruments need an internet connection the first time you use them; then they work offline (see Working offline).
- Muted tracks, and tracks silenced by another track's Solo, don't play their notes at all, which saves processing power on big songs.
- If playback stutters on a big song, freeze the tracks you aren't changing (the snowflake in each track's header), switch **Playback** to **Stable** in the Inspector's Master tab, mute tracks you aren't working on, or switch off effects you don't need. Exporting never stutters, it just takes longer.
- Sounds you load are kept in this browser; a private window may not allow it. Save the project to keep its sounds safe.

- The Erhu is shaped in the app from the VSCO violin, and the Mandolin from the FluidR3 steel-string guitar. The Electric Guitar recording goes through an amp stage in the app, and the Acoustic Guitar (Real) plays the nylon-string guitar voiced brighter. The Violin, Cello and Strings notes are 3 seconds long in the soundfont, so the app loops their steady part when it loads them and a note can be held as long as you like. The top octave of the Violin, Erhu, Strings and Flute plays quieter so it doesn't pierce.
- The sets marked "hosted with the app" were converted (split into notes, tuned and levelled as described above) and are served with Fledgetone; the others load straight from the projects above, unchanged.
